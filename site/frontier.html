The frontier

Silicon is close to the most it can ever do. The next gains come from new materials, from stacking two cells into one, and from catching light on both sides of a panel.

A single solar cell has one band gap. Photons with less energy than the gap pass straight through. Photons with more give up their extra energy as heat. That is why no single-material cell can beat about 33 % in ordinary sunlight, the Shockley–Queisser limit. The best silicon cell is at 28.29 %, 2026-09-30 and silicon's own practical ceiling is about 29.4 %. To go much further, a cell has to waste less of each photon.

Perovskites

Active research

A perovskite is any crystal with the same pattern of atoms as the mineral of that name. The ones used in solar cells are metal-halide perovskites, typically lead, iodine and a small organic molecule. They absorb light very strongly, so a layer a few hundred nanometres thick is enough, and they can be printed or coated from a liquid at low temperature instead of grown from molten silicon.

The first perovskite solar cell, made in 2009 by Tsutomu Miyasaka's group in Japan, turned 3.8 % of light into electricity. It used a liquid electrolyte and did not last long. In 2012 teams in Korea and Switzerland made a solid version above 9 %, and the field took off. Small perovskite cells have since reached 28.0 % in the independent efficiency tables, above the record for multicrystalline silicon and close behind single-crystal silicon. 2026-10-02 Watch the climb on efficiency over time.

Two problems stand between a record and a roof:

  • Lifetime. Silicon panels are expected to work for 25 years or more. Heat, moisture, oxygen and even light itself slowly break perovskites down. Labs now test them under agreed ageing procedures so results can be compared, but decades of outdoor data do not exist yet.
  • Lead. The best perovskites contain a small amount of lead in a form that dissolves in water if a panel breaks. Researchers study ways to seal it in and recycle it, and lead-free (tin-based) versions, which so far are less efficient and raise their own toxicity questions.

Two cells in one: tandems

A tandem cell stacks two cells. The top one has a wide band gap: it takes the high-energy blue and green photons and, because its gap is wide, keeps more of each one's energy. The rest of the light passes through to a narrower-gap cell underneath, which takes the red and near-infrared. Silicon makes a natural bottom cell, and a perovskite, whose band gap can be tuned by changing its recipe, a natural top cell.

The catch is wiring. In the common two-terminal design the two cells are connected in series, so the same current flows through both. Whichever cell makes less current sets the current for the pair. Designers therefore pick the band gaps so that both make the same current; this is current matching.

Current matching, in one line

In series, currents cannot add but voltages do. The stack makes the voltage of both cells together, at a current no larger than the smaller of the two.

Choose a 1.50 eV top cell instead and it swallows too much light. Silicon gets only the thin slice between 1.12 and 1.50 eV, makes half the current, and drags the whole stack down to 24.5 %: worse than silicon on its own.

Not modelled: luminescent coupling (light re-emitted by the top cell and caught by the bottom), parasitic absorption in the contact layers, and the real AM1.5 spectrum, whose absorption dips shift the optimum gaps.

Real tandems are catching up with the model. LONGi's perovskite-on-silicon cell was certified at 35.5 % in July 2026. 2026-09-30 The same company reported 34.3 % on a 261 cm² cell, the size of a factory wafer, and 33 % at that size had already entered the efficiency tables in 2025. The first two-terminal perovskite/silicon cell was reported only in 2015. Whether such stacks can last as long as plain silicon is the same open question as for perovskites alone.

Light from behind: bifacial modules

Current consensus

A normal panel has an opaque back sheet. A bifacial module uses glass, or a clear sheet, on both faces and cells that work from either side, so it also catches sunlight reflected from the ground. How much extra it collects depends on how bright the ground is (its albedo), how high the panel stands and how the rows are spaced. A rooftop panel lying flat on the roof gains almost nothing; a raised panel over white gravel or snow gains a lot.

The idea is old: in 1982 a Spanish team reported up to 50 % more output from a flat panel collecting light reflected onto its back. What changed is cost. A worldwide study found that bifacial modules on single-axis trackers, which turn to follow the sun, make about 35 % more energy than ordinary fixed panels and give the cheapest electricity across 93 % of the world's land area.

Other paths

Active research

  • III-V multijunction cells stack three to six layers of gallium arsenide and related crystals. They hold the absolute record, 47.6 % under 665 suns of concentrated light, and 39.2 % for a six-junction cell in ordinary sunlight. They are too costly for rooftops, so they power satellites, and concentrator systems that focus sunlight onto a tiny cell.
  • Organic cells use carbon-based molecules that can be printed on flexible plastic. They passed 19 % in 2022.
  • Quantum-dot cells are made from nanocrystals whose band gap depends on their size, so one material can be tuned across the spectrum; a cell certified at 16.6 % was reported in 2020.

Beyond junction-stacking. A hot-carrier cell would extract electrons before they lose their excess energy to the crystal lattice; Ross and Nozik's 1982 analysis showed such a converter could in principle far exceed a single junction. An intermediate-band cell adds an energy level inside the gap so two sub-gap photons can together lift one electron; Luque and Martí predicted it could beat a single-gap cell by a wide margin. Neither has yet produced a high-efficiency device. Both remain proofs of what physics allows, not products.

For any number of junctions there is still a ceiling. De Vos calculated in 1980 that an infinite stack of cells, each taking one sliver of the spectrum, could reach about 68 % in ordinary sunlight and about 86 % in sunlight concentrated as far as physics allows. Those limits come from thermodynamics: a cell at room temperature must lose some energy back to its surroundings, just like any heat engine.

De Vos's numbers use a blackbody sun, the same model as the tandem designer above: about 30 %, 42 % and 49 % for one, two and three cells unconcentrated, and 40 %, 55 % and 63 % at maximum concentration. Henry repeated the calculation for the terrestrial spectrum the same year. LONGi puts the practical ceiling for its perovskite/silicon design at about 43 %.
