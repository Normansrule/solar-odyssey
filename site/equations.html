Light and the cell

Photon energy

Light arrives in packets called photons. The shorter the wavelength, the more energy each packet carries: blue light comes in bigger packets than red, and infrared in smaller ones still.

E = h f = \frac{h c}{\lambda}
E
energy of one photon J or eV
h
Planck constant, 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s
f
frequency of the light Hz
c
speed of light, 2.998 × 10⁸ m/s
\lambda
wavelength m (or nm)

Photon energies are tiny in joules, so we use the electronvolt. With λ in nanometres the formula becomes E\,[\text{eV}] \approx 1239.84 / \lambda\,[\text{nm}].

Green light at 500 nm: E = 1239.84 / 500 = 2.48 eV per photon.

The 1239.84 comes from hc/q with the exact 2019 SI values of h, c and the elementary charge q, times 10⁹ to turn metres into nanometres. A photon of 500 nm has frequency 6.0 × 10¹⁴ Hz and energy 3.97 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.

In joules: 3.97 × 10⁻¹⁹ J, which is 2.48 eV divided by 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ J/eV.

The band-gap condition

A solar cell's material has a band gap: the smallest energy that frees an electron to carry current. A photon is absorbed and makes a free electron only if its energy is at least the gap. Anything with less energy passes straight through, and anything with more loses its surplus as heat.

\text{absorbed} \iff E \ge E_g, \qquad \lambda_{\max} = \frac{hc}{E_g}
E
photon energy eV
E_g
band gap of the absorber eV
\lambda_{\max}
longest wavelength that can be absorbed nm
Silicon's gap is about 1.12 eV, so \lambda_{\max} = 1239.84 / 1.12 = 1107 nm, in the near infrared. A 1200 nm photon carries only 1.03 eV and is not absorbed.
A violet 400 nm photon (3.10 eV) is absorbed, but 1.98 eV of it, more than half, is lost as heat.

The single-diode equation

A lit solar cell behaves like a current source (the light) wired in parallel with a diode. Light pushes a steady current out; the diode leaks some of it back inside, and the leak grows very fast as the voltage rises. What reaches your wires is the difference. Plotting it gives the I-V curve.

I = I_{ph} - I_0\left(e^{V/(n V_t)} - 1\right), \qquad V_t = \frac{k_B T}{q}
I
current delivered by the cell A
I_{ph}
current generated by light A
I_0
diode saturation current A
V
voltage across the cell V
n
ideality factor, 1 for an ideal diode none
V_t
thermal voltage, 25.7 mV at 25 °C V
k_B,\ T,\ q
Boltzmann constant, cell temperature, electron charge J/K, K, C
At 25 °C (298.15 K): V_t = k_B T / q = 25.69 mV.
A full-size silicon cell with I_{ph} = 9.72 A and I_0 = 1.215 × 10⁻¹¹ A, held at 0.6 V: the diode leaks back 0.17 A, so the cell delivers 9.55 A.

Real cells add a series resistance R_s (contacts, fingers) and a shunt resistance R_{sh} (leakage paths). The current then appears on both sides, so the site solves it numerically with Newton's method:

I = I_{ph} - I_0\left(e^{(V + I R_s)/(n V_t)} - 1\right) - \frac{V + I R_s}{R_{sh}}

Open-circuit voltage

Disconnect the wires and no current can leave, so the voltage climbs until the diode leak swallows all the light current. That voltage is the open-circuit voltage. Because of the logarithm, ten times more light adds only a small, fixed step in voltage.

V_{oc} = n V_t \ln\!\left(\frac{I_{ph}}{I_0} + 1\right)
V_{oc}
open-circuit voltage V
n V_t
ideality factor times thermal voltage V
I_{ph},\ I_0
light current and saturation current A
Same cell: V_{oc} = 0.02569 \times \ln(8.0\times 10^{11}) = 0.704 V.

Each factor of 10 in light adds n V_t \ln 10 \approx 59 mV at 25 °C, which is why concentrator cells gain voltage and why dim light costs less voltage than you might expect.

Fill factor

A cell cannot deliver its full current and its full voltage at the same time. The fill factor says how much of the ideal box, V_{oc}\times I_{sc}, the best operating point actually fills. A square-shouldered I-V curve means a high fill factor.

FF = \frac{P_{mp}}{V_{oc}\, I_{sc}} = \frac{V_{mp}\, I_{mp}}{V_{oc}\, I_{sc}}
FF
fill factor none (0 to 1)
P_{mp}
power at the maximum power point W
V_{mp},\ I_{mp}
voltage and current at that point V, A
I_{sc}
short-circuit current A
The ideal cell above peaks at 0.621 V and 9.33 A, or 5.80 W. So FF = 5.80 / (0.704 \times 9.72) = 0.847.
Add a realistic series resistance (2.1 mΩ) and shunt resistance (21 Ω) and the shoulder rounds off: 0.819.

Efficiency

Efficiency is electrical power out divided by sunlight power in. Cells are compared under standard test conditions: 1000 W/m² of light with the AM1.5 spectrum, and the cell at 25 °C.

\eta = \frac{P_{out}}{P_{in}} = \frac{V_{oc}\, I_{sc}\, FF}{G \, A}
\eta
power-conversion efficiency none (or %)
P_{out}
electrical power at the maximum power point W
G
irradiance on the cell W/m²
A
cell area m²
The ideal cell above gives 5.80 W from a 243 cm² (0.0243 m²) cell under 1000 W/m²: \eta = 5.80 / (1000 \times 0.0243) = 23.9 %.

The Shockley–Queisser limit

Put the band-gap rule and the diode together and a ceiling appears. A small gap absorbs almost every photon but wastes most of each one's energy as heat and gives a low voltage. A large gap gives a high voltage but lets most photons through. Even a perfect single-junction cell also has to glow back a little light, as every warm object does. Balancing these losses gives the Shockley–Queisser limit. Current consensus

\eta_{\max}(E_g) = \frac{\max_V \; V\left[J_{sc}(E_g) - J_0(E_g)\left(e^{V/V_t} - 1\right)\right]}{P_{sun}}
J_{sc}(E_g)
charge per second from every sunlight photon above the gap, per area A/m²
J_0(E_g)
the cell's own thermal glow above the gap, as a current density A/m²
P_{sun}
incoming sunlight power per area W/m²

Shockley and Queisser treated the Sun as a 6000 K blackbody and found a maximum of 30 % for a gap of 1.1 eV. Using the real AM1.5G reference spectrum, Rühle's tabulation gives a maximum of 33.16 % at a gap of 1.34 eV. You will often see 33.7 % quoted instead; the small difference comes from the spectrum data and assumptions used. Either way, a single junction under ordinary sunlight cannot turn much more than a third of the light into electricity.

Our blackbody version, with a 6000 K Sun as in 1961 and a 1.1 eV gap, gives 30.0 %, matching the original paper. The curve rises gently to about 31 % near 1.3 eV.
With the Sun's modern effective temperature (5772 K) and a 1.34 eV gap: 30.4 %. That is lower than 33.16 % because a blackbody is a rougher stand-in for sunlight at the ground than the AM1.5G spectrum.

Assumptions of the detailed-balance limit: every photon above the gap makes exactly one electron-hole pair; carriers cool to the band edges instantly; the only recombination is radiative; the cell is at 300 K and sees the unconcentrated Sun. Break any of these (tandems, concentration, hot carriers, multiple exciton generation) and the ceiling moves, which is why multijunction cells exceed it. Active research

In the site's model, J_{sc} = q f_\omega \int_{E_g}^{\infty} \phi_{bb}(E, T_{sun})\, dE and J_0 = q\int_{E_g}^{\infty} \phi_{bb}(E, T_{cell})\, dE, where \phi_{bb} = \frac{2\pi}{h^3c^2}\frac{E^2}{e^{E/k_BT} - 1} is the blackbody photon flux into a hemisphere and f_\omega = (R_\odot / 1\,\text{au})^2 dilutes it to what reaches Earth.

Sunlight and yield

Irradiance and energy yield

Irradiance is power: how many watts of sunlight land on each square metre right now. Add it up over a day and you get energy in kWh/m². Divide that by the 1000 W/m² reference and you have peak sun hours: the day squeezed into full-strength hours. A panel's rating in kWp is its output at 1000 W/m², so the day's energy follows directly, trimmed by a performance ratio for heat, wiring, inverter and dirt losses.

E_{day} = P_{STC} \times PSH \times PR, \qquad E_{year} \approx 365 \times E_{day}
G
irradiance W/m²
P_{STC}
array rating at 1000 W/m² kWp
PSH
peak sun hours: daily insolation in kWh/m² h per day
PR
performance ratio, typically 0.75 to 0.85 none
E_{day},\ E_{year}
energy delivered kWh
A 5 kWp roof, 4.5 peak sun hours, PR 0.8: 5 \times 4.5 \times 0.8 = 18 kWh per day.
Over a year of such days (1642.5 peak sun hours): 6570 kWh. Real years vary with season and weather.

Air mass

When the Sun is low, its light crosses more atmosphere and arrives dimmer and redder. Air mass counts how many atmospheres thick that path is: 1 with the Sun straight overhead, about 1.5 at 48° from overhead (the AM1.5 used to test cells), and about 2 at 60°.

AM \approx \frac{1}{\cos\theta_z} \quad\text{(flat Earth)}, \qquad AM = \frac{1}{\cos\theta_z + 0.50572\,(96.07995^\circ - \theta_z)^{-1.6364}}
AM
relative optical air mass none
\theta_z
solar zenith angle, measured from straight up degrees
At a zenith angle of 48.19°: AM 1.50, the standard test spectrum's air mass.
At 60°: AM 1.99. The simple 1/\cos\theta_z gives exactly 2.

The plain secant formula treats the atmosphere as a flat slab and blows up at the horizon. Kasten and Young's fit adds Earth's curvature and refraction, so it stays finite (about 38) at the horizon.

Angle of incidence

A panel catches the most direct sunlight when it faces the Sun square on. Tilt it away and the same beam spreads over more surface, so each square metre gets less: the beam is multiplied by the cosine of the angle of incidence.

G_{beam} = DNI \cdot \cos\theta, \qquad \cos\theta = \cos\theta_z\cos\beta + \sin\theta_z\sin\beta\cos(\gamma_s - \gamma)
DNI
direct normal irradiance, the beam measured facing the Sun W/m²
\theta
angle between the sunbeam and the panel's normal degrees
\theta_z,\ \gamma_s
solar zenith angle and solar azimuth degrees
\beta,\ \gamma
panel tilt from horizontal and panel azimuth degrees
A south-facing panel tilted 30°, with the Sun 50° from overhead and 45° west of south: cos θ = 0.828, so the panel gets 83 % of the direct beam.

Money and learning

Capacity factor

A plant's nameplate says what it can produce flat out. The capacity factor says what share of that it actually delivered over a year, after night, weather, maintenance and low demand.

CF = \frac{E_{year}}{P_{rated} \times 8760\ \text{h}}
CF
capacity factor none (0 to 1)
E_{year}
energy produced in a year MWh
P_{rated}
nameplate capacity MW
8760
hours in a (non-leap) year h
A 100 MW plant that delivers 219 000 MWh in a year: 219\,000 / (100 \times 8760) = 0.25, or 25 %.

Levelized cost of energy

The levelized cost of energy is the price per MWh a plant would have to earn, every year of its life, to pay back what it cost to build and run, including the return its investors expect. The capital recovery factor turns the up-front cost into an equal yearly payment, like a mortgage.

LCOE = \frac{CAPEX \cdot CRF + FOM}{8.76 \cdot CF} + VOM + \text{fuel}, \qquad CRF = \frac{r(1+r)^n}{(1+r)^n - 1}
CAPEX
up-front capital cost $/kW
FOM
fixed operation and maintenance $/kW per year
VOM, fuel
variable running and fuel costs $/MWh
CF
capacity factor none
8.76
MWh per kW per year at CF = 1 MWh/kW
r,\ n
real discount rate and economic lifetime fraction, years

Assumptions in this example. The inputs are round illustrative numbers chosen to show the arithmetic, not current market prices: $1000/kW to build, $20/kW a year to run, no fuel, 20 % capacity factor, 7 % real discount rate, 25-year life, no taxes or subsidies. For real, dated costs see The cost collapse and State of the field.

At 7 % over 25 years: CRF = 0.0858, so each $1000 of capital costs $85.81 a year.
(1000 \times 0.0858 + 20) / (8.76 \times 0.2) = $60.39 per MWh, about 6 cents per kWh.

The general definition discounts every year's cost and every year's energy: LCOE = \sum_t \frac{C_t}{(1+r)^t} \Big/ \sum_t \frac{E_t}{(1+r)^t}. With level yearly costs and output it reduces to the capital-recovery form above. LCOE ignores when the energy arrives and what it is worth then, which matters for variable sources; see The smart grid.

Wright's law

The more of something the world has made, the cheaper the next one gets. Wright's law says each doubling of cumulative production cuts the cost by a fixed share, the learning rate. It was first described for aircraft in 1936 and has since fitted many technologies, solar modules among them.

C = C_0\,(1 - LR)^{d}, \qquad d = \log_2\frac{Q}{Q_0}
C,\ C_0
cost now and at the starting point $ per unit
LR
learning rate: fractional cost drop per doubling none
Q,\ Q_0
cumulative production now and at the start units (e.g. GW)
d
number of doublings none
A 20 % learning rate over 10 doublings (about a thousandfold more production): 100 \times 0.8^{10} = $10.74, roughly a tenth of the start.

Wind and water

Wind power

Wind is moving air, and moving mass carries kinetic energy. The power flowing through a rotor's disc grows with the cube of the wind speed: the air is faster and more of it arrives each second. Double the wind and you get eight times the power.

P_{wind} = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho A v^3, \qquad P_{rotor} = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho A v^3\, C_p, \qquad A = \pi\left(\tfrac{D}{2}\right)^2
\rho
air density, about 1.225 at sea level and 15 °C kg/m³
A,\ D
swept area and rotor diameter m², m
v
wind speed m/s
C_p
power coefficient: share captured none
Through one square metre at 10 m/s: 0.5 \times 1.225 \times 1 \times 10^3 = 612.5 W.
A 120 m rotor with C_p = 0.45 at 10 m/s: 3.12 MW.

The Betz limit

A rotor cannot stop the wind completely, or the air would pile up and nothing more could flow through. It cannot let it pass untouched either, or it would catch nothing. The best compromise slows the wind by a third at the rotor, which captures 16/27 of the power: the Betz limit, about 59.3 %. Settled physics

C_p = 4a(1-a)^2, \qquad C_{p,\max} = C_p\!\left(\tfrac{1}{3}\right) = \frac{16}{27} \approx 0.593
a
axial induction factor: fraction by which the rotor slows the wind none
C_p
power coefficient of an ideal actuator disc none
At a = 1/3: 4 \times \tfrac13 \times \left(\tfrac23\right)^2 = \tfrac{16}{27} = 0.593.

Momentum theory: the wind slows from v far upstream to v(1-a) at the disc and v(1-2a) far downstream. Power is thrust times disc velocity, P = 2\rho A v^3 a(1-a)^2; setting dP/da = 0 gives a = 1/3. The history of who derived it first (Lanchester, Betz, Joukowsky) is reviewed in the cited paper.

Hydropower

Falling water gives up its height energy. The power is how much water falls each second, times how far it falls (the head), times gravity, less what the turbine and generator lose.

P = \eta\, \rho\, g\, Q\, H
\eta
turbine and generator efficiency none
\rho
water density, about 1000 kg/m³
g
gravitational acceleration, 9.81 m/s²
Q
flow through the turbines m³/s
H
head: height the water drops m
100 m³/s falling 50 m at 90 % efficiency: 0.9 \times 1000 \times 9.81 \times 100 \times 50 = 44.1 MW.

Tidal basin energy

A tidal barrage traps high water behind a wall and lets it out through turbines at low tide. The energy grows with the square of the tidal range: a bigger range means both more water and a longer fall.

E = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho\, g\, A\, R^2
E
energy released by emptying the basin once J
\rho
seawater density, about 1025 kg/m³
A
basin area m²
R
tidal range, high water minus low water m
A 10 km² basin with an 8 m range: 3.2 × 10¹² J, or about 890 MWh per emptying, before turbine losses.

Why the ½: the water column of height R has its centre of mass at R/2, so its mass \rho A R falls an average of R/2.

Wave energy flux

Ocean waves carry energy toward the shore. The power arriving along each metre of wave crest grows with the square of the wave height and in step with the wave period: tall, slow swells carry the most.

P = \frac{\rho\, g^2\, H_s^2\, T_e}{64\pi}
P
power per metre of wave crest W/m
H_s
significant wave height m
T_e
energy period of the waves s
\rho,\ g
seawater density and gravity kg/m³, m/s²
Waves of 2 m with an 8 s period: 15.7 kW along every metre of crest.

This is the deep-water result, where the water depth exceeds about half a wavelength. In shallow water the waves slow down and the flux formula changes.

Heat and storage

Carnot efficiency

Any engine that turns heat into work, whether in a geothermal, solar-thermal or biomass plant, must dump some heat into a colder place. The hotter the source compared with the sink, the more work you can get. Carnot efficiency is the ceiling no real engine reaches. Settled physics

\eta_C = 1 - \frac{T_c}{T_h}
T_h
hot-side temperature, in kelvin (°C + 273.15) K
T_c
cold-side temperature K
Heat at 565 °C (838 K) rejected at 40 °C (313 K): 62.6 % at most.
A cooler source at 200 °C, rejected at 30 °C: 35.9 %, which is why low-temperature geothermal converts so little of its heat.

Round-trip efficiency

Storing energy and getting it back always costs something. Each step loses a share, and the shares multiply: the round-trip efficiency is the product of every stage's efficiency, so a long chain of good steps can still add up to a poor whole.

\eta_{rt} = \frac{E_{out}}{E_{in}} = \eta_1 \times \eta_2 \times \cdots \times \eta_k
E_{in},\ E_{out}
energy put in and taken back out kWh
\eta_i
efficiency of stage i none
Charging at 95 % and discharging at 95 %: 90.3 % comes back.
A two-step chain of 70 % and 55 %: only 38.5 % returns.
