How a cell works

The p-n junction

Light can free an electron in silicon, but a freed electron alone makes no current. A solar cell needs a built-in push that sends electrons one way and holes the other. That push lives in a layer thinner than a soap bubble, where two kinds of silicon meet.

In the photovoltaic effect lesson, a photon with enough energy lifted an electron out of its bond. It left behind a hole, a missing electron that moves through the crystal like a positive charge. Together they form an electron–hole pair. In a plain lump of silicon the pair wanders about for a few millionths of a second and then the electron drops back into a hole. That is recombination, and the energy ends up as a little heat. To get electricity out, something has to pull the pair apart and steer each half to a different wire.

Two kinds of silicon

Pure silicon has four outer electrons per atom, and every one of them is locked in a bond with a neighbour. Engineers change that by doping: mixing in a tiny, controlled amount of another element.

  • Phosphorus has five outer electrons. Four fit into bonds; the fifth is barely held and wanders off at room temperature. Silicon doped this way is n-type (n for the negative electrons it adds).
  • Boron has only three. One bond around each boron atom is missing an electron: a hole. Silicon doped this way is p-type (p for positive).

Either way the crystal stays electrically neutral. Each phosphorus atom that gives away an electron becomes a fixed positive ion; each boron atom that accepts one becomes a fixed negative ion. The ions cannot move. Only the electrons and holes can.

Silicon packs about 5.0 × 10²² atoms into each cubic centimetre. A typical solar-cell base is doped with 10¹⁶ boron atoms per cm³, which is one boron atom for every 5 million silicon atoms. A pinch of salt in a swimming pool changes how the whole pool conducts.

When p meets n

Now make one crystal that is p-type on one side and n-type on the other. The boundary is the p-n junction. Three things happen within a fraction of a second.

  1. Diffusion. The n side is crowded with free electrons and the p side with holes. Carriers spread out from where they are crowded, as a drop of ink spreads in water. This is diffusion. Electrons cross into the p side, holes into the n side, and there they meet and cancel.
  2. A depleted layer. Near the boundary almost no free carriers remain. What is left are the fixed ions: negative boron ions on the p side, positive phosphorus ions on the n side. This thin strip is the depletion region.
  3. A built-in field. Positive charge on one side and negative on the other make an electric field that points from n to p. It pushes electrons back toward the n side and holes back toward the p side. This push is called drift. Diffusion and drift soon balance, and the flow stops. The voltage step the field creates is the built-in potential, about 0.9 volts for the doping used in this lesson.

Nothing leaves the junction in the dark: the built-in voltage cannot be measured with a voltmeter, because the contacts cancel it. Light is what turns the junction into a source.

How light makes a current

Follow one photon in the explorer.

  1. Generation. It is absorbed and makes an electron–hole pair, usually somewhere in the thick p-type base.
  2. A random walk. The electron is a rare visitor there (a minority carrier). It diffuses about at random. If it recombines before finding the junction, its energy is lost as heat.
  3. Separation. If it reaches the edge of the depletion region, the built-in field grabs it and sweeps it across to the n side. Holes that reach the junction from the n side are swept the other way. The field sorts the charges: electrons to n, holes to p.
  4. Collection. Electrons pile up on the n side and holes on the p side. Connect a wire through a load and the extra electrons flow around the circuit, do work in the load, and arrive at the p side to fill holes.

The load decides how much of that current escapes. With a short circuit (zero resistance) every collected electron leaves at once. Current is at its highest and voltage is zero, so no power is delivered. With an open circuit (no path at all) the separated charges build up, pushing back against the built-in field. The barrier drops until as many carriers leak back across the junction and recombine as the light creates. Voltage is at its highest but the current is zero. Somewhere between lies the load that takes the most power, the maximum power point of the next lesson.

You can see the voltage in the band diagram. Energy is drawn upward, and electrons roll downhill while holes float upward. In short circuit the Fermi levels at the two contacts sit at the same height. Under load it splits: the electrons' level on the n side rises above the holes' level on the p side by exactly the cell voltage times the electron's charge.

How tall is the barrier?

The more heavily each side is doped, the bigger the difference in carrier crowding, and the taller the step that holds the carriers apart. The height grows only with the logarithm of the doping: ten times more doping on one side adds about 60 millivolts.

V_{bi} = \frac{kT}{q}\,\ln\!\left(\frac{N_A N_D}{n_i^{2}}\right)
V_{bi}
built-in potential, volts (V)
kT/q
thermal voltage: Boltzmann constant × temperature ÷ electron charge, 25.9 mV at 300 K
N_A
acceptor (boron) density on the p side, per cm³
N_D
donor (phosphorus) density on the n side, per cm³
n_i
intrinsic carrier density of pure silicon: 9.65 × 10⁹ per cm³ at 300 K
A base with N_A = 10^{16}\ \text{cm}^{-3} and an emitter with N_D = 10^{19}\ \text{cm}^{-3} at 300 K: V_{bi} = 0.02585 \times \ln(10^{35} / 9.31\times10^{19}) \approx 0.02585 \times 34.6 = 0.895 V.

In the explorer's band diagram this is the height of the step in the band edges when the cell is short-circuited. Under load the step shrinks to V_{bi} - V.

The value of n_i has been revised as measurements improved. Older textbooks quote noticeably higher values; Sproul and Green measured 1.00 × 10¹⁰ cm⁻³ at 300 K; Altermatt and colleagues, correcting for band-gap narrowing, gave 9.65 × 10⁹ cm⁻³. This page uses the last. Because V_{bi} depends on \ln n_i^2, switching from 10¹⁰ to 9.65 × 10⁹ moves it by only 2 mV.

The formula assumes non-degenerate doping (Boltzmann statistics) and an abrupt step from p to n. Real diffused emitters have graded profiles and doping above about 10¹⁹ cm⁻³ narrows the band gap, so treat the numbers as textbook estimates.

How wide is the depletion region?

The field has to be made of uncovered ions. Lightly doped silicon has few ions per cubic centimetre, so it must uncover a thicker layer to build the same barrier. That is why the depletion region reaches mostly into the lightly doped base. Forward voltage, as under load, lowers the barrier and thins the layer.

W = \sqrt{\frac{2\varepsilon\,(V_{bi} - V)}{q}\,\frac{N_A + N_D}{N_A N_D}}
W
depletion width, metres (quoted here in micrometres, µm)
\varepsilon
permittivity of silicon, 11.7 × 8.854 × 10⁻¹² F/m
V
voltage across the junction, positive for forward bias, volts
q
elementary charge, 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C
For the same junction in the dark, W \approx 0.34 µm, hundreds of times thinner than the wafer, which is well over a hundred micrometres thick. Almost all of it lies on the lightly doped p side.

Charge neutrality fixes the split: the uncovered charge is equal on both sides, N_A x_p = N_D x_n, so x_n = W N_A/(N_A+N_D) and x_p = W N_D/(N_A+N_D). The field rises linearly through each side and peaks at the metallurgical junction:

E_{max} = \frac{q N_D x_n}{\varepsilon} = \frac{2\,(V_{bi} - V)}{W}
With x_p = 0.340\ \mu\text{m} and x_n = 0.34\ \text{nm}, the peak field is 5.3 × 10⁴ V/cm: over five million volts per metre, across a layer a few hundred times thinner than a hair.

A seam in a silicon slab

Settled history

  1. 1940At Bell Labs, Russell Ohl was testing samples of purified silicon. On 23 February he found that light falling on one rod, which had a visible seam across it, produced a surprisingly large voltage. With his colleague Jack Scaff he traced the effect to impurities that had separated as the melt froze: phosphorus on one side, boron on the other. They named the regions n-type and p-type, and the boundary the p-n junction.
  2. 1941–1946Ohl filed a patent for a “light-sensitive electric device” in May 1941; it was granted in June 1946 as US 2,402,662. It describes a silicon cell with an internal barrier that makes a voltage when lit.
  3. 1954Daryl Chapin, Calvin Fuller and Gerald Pearson, also at Bell Labs, made a p-n junction just under the surface of a silicon wafer. It turned about 6 % of sunlight into electricity, far better than anything before. Bell Labs announced the cell on 25 April 1954.

Every silicon cell sold today still works on the same principle. What changed is how thin, clean and well passivated the layers are, which you can compare in cell technologies today. The whole story is on the timeline.
