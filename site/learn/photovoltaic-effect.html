How a cell works, lesson 1

The photovoltaic effect

Light arrives in packets, and a packet with enough energy frees an electron.

A solar cell has no moving parts, yet it turns sunlight straight into electric current. The trick happens one tiny packet of light at a time. Each packet either has enough energy to free an electron inside the cell, or it does not. Nothing in between.

Those packets are called photons. A photon's energy depends only on its colour, which physicists describe by its wavelength: the shorter the wavelength, the bigger the packet. Blue photons carry more energy than red ones, and infrared photons, which our eyes cannot see, carry less still.

What happens inside

In a semiconductor such as silicon, almost every electron is busy holding atoms together. These bound electrons sit in a range of energies called the valence band. Above it, separated by a forbidden stretch of energy, is the conduction band, where an electron is free to move and carry current. The forbidden stretch is the band gap.

A photon that reaches an electron hands over all of its energy or none of it. If the photon carries at least the band-gap energy, the electron jumps into the conduction band. It leaves behind an empty spot in the bond, a hole, which behaves like a positive charge and can move too. Together they make an electron–hole pair. If the photon carries less than the gap, there is no allowed energy for the electron to land on, so nothing happens and the photon passes straight through.

A photon with more energy than the gap does not do extra work. The electron lands high in the conduction band, then quickly shakes off the surplus as vibrations of the crystal, which is heat. This is thermalisation, and it is the single biggest loss in a solar cell. Watch the amber squiggles in the widget: a violet photon in silicon throws more than half of its energy away.

Freeing electrons is only half the job. Left alone, an electron soon falls back into a hole and the energy is lost again (recombination). A working cell builds in an electric field that pushes electrons one way and holes the other, so they flow out through a circuit as current. That field comes from the p-n junction, the next lesson but one; first, the band gap explains why each material wastes sunlight differently.

Absorption must conserve both energy and crystal momentum. A photon carries almost no momentum, so in a direct-gap material (GaAs, CdTe, halide perovskites) the electron goes straight up. Silicon and germanium have an indirect gap: the lowest conduction-band states sit at a different momentum from the top of the valence band, so a lattice vibration (phonon) must take part. Near the gap that makes absorption weak, and silicon needs a much thicker absorber.

In an ordinary cell one photon makes at most one pair, which is what the widget and the Shockley–Queisser limit assume. Turning the surplus of a high-energy photon into a second pair (carrier multiplication, or singlet fission in molecular layers) is studied as a way around thermalisation. Active research

The energy of a photon

Light is a wave, and the energy of each photon is proportional to how fast the wave oscillates, its frequency. Because all light travels at the same speed, frequency and wavelength are tied together: halve the wavelength and you double the frequency, and the energy.

E = h f = \frac{h c}{\lambda}
E
energy of one photon, in joules (J) or electronvolts (eV); 1 eV = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ J
h
Planck constant, 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s (exact by definition since 2019)
f
frequency of the light, in hertz (Hz)
c
speed of light, 299 792 458 m/s
\lambda
wavelength, in metres (here usually nanometres, nm)

A handy shortcut: hc \approx 1240\ \text{eV·nm}, so the energy in electronvolts is 1240 divided by the wavelength in nanometres.

A green photon of wavelength 500 nm carries 1240 ÷ 500 ≈ 2.48 eV.
In joules that is 2.48 × 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ ≈ 3.97 × 10⁻¹⁹ J: tiny, which is why sunlight is so many photons.
Its frequency is c ÷ λ = 2.998 × 10⁸ ÷ 500 × 10⁻⁹ ≈ 6.00 × 10¹⁴ Hz (600 THz).
Turn the formula round to find the longest wavelength silicon (gap 1.12 eV) can absorb: λ = 1240 ÷ 1.12 ≈ 1107 nm, in the near infrared. Anything redder passes through.
A 500 nm photon absorbed by silicon keeps 1.12 eV and loses 2.48 − 1.12 ≈ 1.36 eV as heat.
So only 1.12 ÷ 2.48 ≈ 45 % of that photon's energy can ever reach the circuit.
A 1 mW beam of 500 nm light carries 0.001 ÷ 3.97 × 10⁻¹⁹ ≈ 2.5 × 10¹⁵ photons per second.

Two effects, two centuries

Settled history In 1839 the 19-year-old Edmond Becquerel shone light on two metal plates dipped in a conducting solution and measured a small current that appeared when the light fell on them: the first recorded photovoltaic effect.

Half a century later Heinrich Hertz noticed that ultraviolet light helps sparks jump between metal electrodes. Philipp Lenard then showed that light knocks electrons out of a metal surface, and that their energy depends on the light's colour, not its brightness. In 1905 Albert Einstein explained this by treating light as packets, each with energy hf. Robert Millikan's careful measurements confirmed the prediction in 1916, and Einstein's Nobel Prize cites this work on the photoelectric effect.

The two effects are cousins, and easy to mix up. Both start with a photon handing its energy to an electron. What differs is where the electron goes.

Einstein's packet picture is what makes the photovoltaic effect make sense: a single photon either clears the band gap or it does not, however bright the light. Brighter light only means more photons, and so more pairs and more current.

For the photoelectric effect Einstein wrote the largest kinetic energy of an ejected electron as K_\text{max} = hf - \phi, with \phi the work function. In a solar cell the electron is not ejected: the condition is simply hf \ge E_g, and the surplus hf - E_g thermalises to the lattice rather than appearing as kinetic energy outside.
