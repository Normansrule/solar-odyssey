How a cell works

Current, voltage and power

A solar cell is not a battery with a fixed voltage. How much current and voltage it gives depends on what you connect to it. One curve sums up everything it can do, and one point on that curve is the one worth chasing.

In the p-n junction lesson you moved a load from short circuit to open circuit and watched the current and voltage trade off. Plot every one of those operating points, current against voltage, and you get the cell's I-V curve. Engineers measure it for every cell and every module that leaves a factory.

Three points on the curve

  • Short circuit. Wire the terminals together. Voltage is zero and the current is the most the cell can push: the short-circuit current, I_{sc}. It is set by how many photons make electron–hole pairs that reach the junction, so it rises in step with the light.
  • Open circuit. Connect nothing. No current flows and the voltage climbs to the open-circuit voltage, V_{oc}, where carriers leak back across the junction as fast as light makes them. For silicon that is about 0.7 V.
  • The knee. Power is current × voltage, and both ends give zero power. In between, the curve bends sharply at a knee, and near that knee is the maximum power point (MPP), where the rectangle under the curve is as big as it can be. The electronics behind a solar panel, maximum power point trackers (MPPT), spend their whole lives hunting for it; see from cell to grid.

Why the curve has that shape

A lit cell is two things in parallel: a current source made by the light, and a diode, the junction itself. At low voltage the diode barely conducts and almost all the light-made current goes out to the load, so the top of the curve is flat. As the voltage rises toward the junction's turn-on point, the diode starts to pass current internally, each extra tenth of a volt multiplying that leak about fifty times. The curve falls off a cliff.

The single-diode equation

The light current minus what leaks back through the diode minus what leaks around the junction gives the current you get.

I = I_{ph} - I_0\left[\exp\!\left(\frac{V + I R_s}{n\,kT/q}\right) - 1\right] - \frac{V + I R_s}{R_{sh}}
I,\ V
current out of the cell (A) and voltage across its terminals (V)
I_{ph}
photocurrent made by the light (A), proportional to irradiance
I_0
dark saturation current (A): the junction's leakage, set by recombination; about 10⁻¹¹ A here
n
ideality factor, 1 for an ideal diode, up to about 2
kT/q
thermal voltage, 25.7 mV at 25 °C
R_s,\ R_{sh}
series and shunt resistance (Ω)

Set I = 0, ignore the resistances, and solve for the voltage:

V_{oc} = \frac{n kT}{q}\,\ln\!\left(\frac{I_{ph}}{I_0} + 1\right)
Our cell makes I_{ph} = 9.72 A at one sun and leaks I_0 = 1.2\times10^{-11} A. Then V_{oc} = 0.02569 \times \ln(8.0\times10^{11}) = 0.02569 \times 27.4 = 0.704 V.

The logarithm explains two everyday facts. Halving the light halves I_{sc} but costs only 18 mV of V_{oc} (kT/q \cdot \ln 2), so cells still give a useful voltage on cloudy days. And cutting the leakage I_0 tenfold, by making cleaner silicon and better surfaces, buys about 59 mV. That is the main story of the cell technologies that followed.

Fill factor: how square is the curve?

Draw the rectangle V_{oc} \times I_{sc}. No cell can deliver that much power, because the two never happen at once. The fill factor says how much of that box the maximum-power rectangle fills. In the lab it is the amber box inside the dashed outline.

FF = \frac{P_{max}}{V_{oc}\,I_{sc}} = \frac{V_{mp}\,I_{mp}}{V_{oc}\,I_{sc}}
P_{max}
maximum power (W), at V_{mp} (V) and I_{mp} (A)
FF
fill factor, a pure number between 0 and 1
Our cell at standard test conditions: P_{max} = 5.60 W, V_{oc} = 0.704 V, I_{sc} = 9.72 A. FF = 5.60 / (0.704 \times 9.72) = 0.818.

Even a perfect diode cannot reach FF = 1, because the exponential knee has a finite sharpness set by kT/q. Green's empirical expression gives the loss-free limit in terms of the normalised voltage v_{oc} = V_{oc}/(nkT/q):

FF_0 = \frac{v_{oc} - \ln(v_{oc} + 0.72)}{v_{oc} + 1}
For V_{oc} = 0.704 V at 25 °C, v_{oc} = 27.4 and FF_0 = 0.847. The lab cell reaches 0.818: its 0.5 Ω·cm² series resistance and 5000 Ω·cm² shunt cost about three points of fill factor.

A rough rule from the same source: FF \approx FF_0 (1 - r_s) with r_s = R_s I_{sc}/V_{oc}, valid for small r_s.

Efficiency

The number on every datasheet is the conversion efficiency: electrical power out divided by sunlight power in. To make it comparable, cells are measured at standard test conditions (STC): 1000 W/m² of light with the AM1.5 global reference spectrum defined in IEC 60904-3, and a cell temperature of 25 °C.

\eta = \frac{P_{max}}{G \cdot A} = \frac{V_{oc}\,I_{sc}\,FF}{G \cdot A}
\eta
efficiency, a fraction (or %)
G
irradiance on the cell (W/m²)
A
cell area (m²)
A 243 cm² cell (0.0243 m²) under 1000 W/m² receives 24.3 W of sunlight. If it delivers 5.6 W at its maximum power point, \eta = 5.6 / 24.3 = 23.0 %.

The lab cell is a solid commercial-grade cell, a little below the best cells in mass production today. The best certified laboratory silicon cell reported so far reached 28.3 % 2026-09-30, against a calculated ceiling of about 29.4 % for a single silicon junction. Records move; the state of the field page keeps the latest. You can follow the records in efficiency over time, and see why there is a ceiling at all in the band gap.

What the resistances do

Real cells lose a little power to the wires inside them.

  • Series resistance sits in the path every electron must take: the silicon, the thin metal fingers printed on the front, the contacts. It eats voltage in proportion to the current, so it tilts the steep right-hand side of the curve and drags the knee down and to the left. Try 3 Ω·cm² in the lab: the fill factor drops from 0.82 to 0.69.
  • Shunt resistance is a leak around the junction, from defects or cell edges. It steals current in proportion to the voltage, tilting the flat top. A healthy cell has thousands of Ω·cm²; at 50 Ω·cm² the fill factor collapses below 0.6.
  • The ideality factor rounds the knee. Values above 1 mean more recombination inside the depletion region. The lab keeps the one-sun, 25 °C voltage fixed as you change it, so you see only the change in shape.
The lab cell with its series resistance raised from 0.5 to 3 Ω·cm² (12 mΩ for 243 cm²): fill factor 0.692, and maximum power down from 5.60 W to 4.73 W.

Why heat lowers the voltage

On a sunny roof a cell runs much hotter than the air around it. Heat shakes more electrons loose from their bonds all by themselves, so the junction leaks much more: I_0 rises steeply. A bigger leak means the voltage at which leakage balances the light-made current is lower. The current rises a little (hot silicon absorbs slightly more of the red end of the spectrum), but far less than the voltage falls.

Warm the lab cell from 25 °C to 50 °C at one sun: V_{oc} falls from 0.704 V to 0.656 V, about 1.9 mV per degree.
As a percentage that is −0.27 % per °C of the voltage. PVEducation quotes about −2.2 mV/°C for typical silicon cells; cells with higher voltage, like this one, are a little less sensitive. The cell's maximum power falls about 0.3 % per °C.

This is why panels make the most power on cold, bright days, and why datasheets list temperature coefficients next to the efficiency.

The temperature law in the lab

For an ideal diode the dark current is proportional to n_i^2, which scales as T^3 \exp(-E_{g0}/kT). Green writes the general form with an exponent \gamma that absorbs the temperature dependence of diffusion constants and lifetimes:

I_0(T) = B\,T^{\gamma} \exp\!\left(-\frac{E_{g0}}{kT}\right)

Here E_{g0} \approx 1.2 eV is silicon's band gap extrapolated linearly to 0 K, and \gamma \approx 3. The lab scales the 25 °C value with this law, divides the exponent by n for non-ideal diodes, and lets the photocurrent rise by 0.06 % per °C. Differentiating V_{oc} gives

\frac{dV_{oc}}{dT} = -\frac{E_{g0}/q - V_{oc} + \gamma kT/q}{T}
With V_{oc} = 0.704 V at 298 K: -(1.2 - 0.704 + 3 \times 0.0257)/298.15 = −1.92 mV/K, matching the lab's finite-difference slope. The unit tests check both.
Ten degrees of warming multiplies the ideal-diode I_0 by 5.0, which costs kT/q \cdot \ln 5 \approx 41 mV, partly offset by the higher kT/q in front of the logarithm.

The model leaves out the slight narrowing of the band gap with temperature and any change in the resistances, both of which matter in precise module modelling.
