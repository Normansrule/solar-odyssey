How a cell works, lesson 2

The band gap

Why silicon eats some colours of sunlight and wastes others.

Think of the band gap as a step that an electron must climb before it can carry current. A photon gives the electron one push, all at once. If the push is too small, the electron stays put and the photon passes through. If the push is bigger than the step, the electron climbs it, but the extra height is lost as heat a moment later. Either way, the height of the step decides how much of each photon's energy the cell can keep.

Sunlight is a mix of photons of every size. So every choice of step height wastes some of it. Choose a low step and almost every photon gets in, but most of each big photon's energy turns to heat. Choose a high step and each absorbed photon keeps a lot, but most photons are too small to climb it. Somewhere in between is a best height.

Why there is a gap at all

An electron alone in space can have any energy. Inside a crystal it cannot. Billions of atoms sit in a regular pattern, and the electron waves bouncing among them only fit at certain energies. Those allowed energies crowd together into broad bands, with forbidden stretches between them.

In a semiconductor at room temperature the valence band is almost completely full: its electrons are holding the crystal together. The conduction band above it is almost empty. The band gap is the distance between the top of the one and the bottom of the other. Lifting an electron across it creates an electron–hole pair; any energy the photon brought beyond the gap is lost to thermalisation, as you saw in the previous lesson. In metals there is no gap, which is why they conduct; in insulators such as glass the gap is so wide that sunlight cannot bridge it.

The bands come from the periodic potential of the lattice. Plotting allowed energy E against crystal momentum \hbar k gives the band structure. If the conduction-band minimum sits at the same k as the valence-band maximum, the gap is direct and a photon alone can drive the transition. GaAs, CdTe, CIGS and halide perovskites are direct.

In silicon and germanium the minimum is displaced in k: the gap is indirect, and absorption near the edge also needs a phonon to supply momentum. That second-order process makes the absorption coefficient small just above the gap, with a long weak tail into the infrared. In silicon, blue light is gone within a few microns while deep-red and near-infrared light close to the gap needs a few hundred, which is why silicon cells are cut from wafers of order a hundred microns thick and use textured surfaces to trap light, while direct-gap thin films do the same job in a few microns. The Shockley–Queisser limit

In 1961 William Shockley and Hans Queisser asked how efficient a perfect single-gap solar cell could be. Their answer rests on detailed balance: anything that absorbs light well must also give light off. A warm cell glows faintly, and once it builds up a voltage it glows more, sending part of its captured energy back out. Add that unavoidable loss to the two you have just seen, and the best possible single cell in sunlight is limited to about 30 %. Settled physics This ceiling is now called the Shockley–Queisser limit.

Why our 30.5 % is not the 33 % you will see quoted

The number usually quoted for the Shockley–Queisser limit uses the standard sea-level spectrum, AM1.5G, instead of a blackbody. Tabulated detailed-balance results for AM1.5G give a maximum of 33.16 % at 1.34 eV. You will also often see 33.7 % repeated; we could not trace that figure to a primary calculation, so we quote the tabulated one.

Our curve is lower because it describes different light. The blackbody sun above the atmosphere delivers about 1361 W/m², including ultraviolet and far-infrared power that no single gap can use well. The atmosphere strips much of that out before sunlight reaches the ground, and AM1.5G is scaled to 1000 W/m², so a larger share of what is left falls where a cell can use it. Its absorption dips also make the AM1.5G curve bumpy, which pulls the best gap to 1.34 eV. Shockley and Queisser themselves used a 6000 K blackbody and found 30 % near 1.1 eV.

For an ideal cell with only radiative recombination, every above-gap photon makes one electron–hole pair that is collected, and the cell emits like a blackbody at its own temperature T_c boosted by e^{qV/kT_c}:

J(V) = J_{sc} - J_0\left(e^{qV/kT_c} - 1\right)
J_{sc} = q\int_{E_g}^{\infty} \Phi_\odot(E)\,dE, \qquad J_0 = q\int_{E_g}^{\infty} \Phi_{bb}(E, T_c)\,dE
\Phi_{bb}(E, T) = \frac{2\pi}{h^3 c^2}\,\frac{E^2}{e^{E/kT} - 1}, \qquad \Phi_\odot(E) = f_\omega\,\Phi_{bb}(E, T_\odot)

Here \Phi are photon fluxes per unit energy (photons m⁻² s⁻¹ J⁻¹), f_\omega = (R_\odot / 1\,\text{au})^2 \approx 2.16 \times 10^{-5} dilutes the sun to Earth's distance, T_\odot = 5772 K and T_c = 300 K. J_{sc} is the short-circuit current and J_0 the radiative dark current. The efficiency is the maximum of VJ(V) divided by f_\omega \sigma T_\odot^4. This is exactly what the chart computes (shockleyQueisser() in physics/pv.js). Real cells fall short through non-radiative recombination, reflection, incomplete absorption and resistance; for how close today's record cells come, see state of the field.

Beating the single-gap limit

The limit applies to one gap in ordinary sunlight. Stack two or more cells with different gaps, wide on top and narrow underneath, and each colour is caught by a gap that wastes little of it. Detailed balance gives about 42 % for two cells and 49 % for three in unconcentrated sunlight, rising towards 68 % for an endless stack. Concentrating the sunlight with lenses or mirrors raises every one of these limits: to about 40 % for a single cell and 86 % for an endless stack at maximum concentration. Settled physics

That is why tandem cells, such as a perovskite cell on top of a silicon one, are one of the most active areas of solar research. The frontier picks up the story.
