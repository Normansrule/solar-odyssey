Sun path and energy yield

The Sun draws a different arc across the sky every day, and a solar panel only catches the light that hits it face on. Pick a place and a date, tilt and turn a panel, and see how much sunlight it collects in a day and in a year.

Everything on this page comes from a few pieces of geometry and one simple model of the atmosphere. The Sun's position is computed from the date, the time and where you stand. The atmosphere dims the beam more when the Sun is low. Then a single cosine decides how much of that beam lands on the panel. The numbers are for a clear sky, so they are the most sunlight a place could get, not what it gets on average. Clear sky is a ceiling explains how real weather changes them.

Reading the sky

Stand outside facing the equator and the Sun climbs from the east, peaks over the equator side of the sky at noon, and sinks to the west. Two angles pin it down at any moment: its elevation above the horizon and its azimuth, the compass direction you would point to.

  • The seasons move the arc. Earth's axis is tilted about 23.4°, so over a year the latitude where the noon Sun stands overhead, its declination, swings between about 23.4° north in June and 23.4° south in December. In your summer the arc is high and long; in winter it is low and short. Compare the red and blue arcs on the dome.
  • The equinox arc is the same everywhere. In March and September the Sun rises due east and sets due west everywhere except at the poles, after about 12 hours. Only the height of the arc changes: the noon Sun stands at 90° minus your latitude.
  • Latitude tilts the whole picture. Pick Quito, almost on the equator: every arc stands nearly upright, and the Sun passes north of overhead in June and south of it in December. Pick Longyearbyen at 78° north: in June the Sun circles the sky without setting, and in December it never rises.

The time slider runs on solar time, the time a sundial would show, so noon is when the Sun is highest. Your clock can be well over an hour away from it, because a time zone is wide, because of daylight saving, and because of the small wobble called the equation of time. For preset places the widget shows the matching standard clock time under the slider.

The position comes from the equations behind NOAA's solar calculator, which follow Meeus's Astronomical Algorithms: the Sun's apparent longitude gives the declination \delta and the equation of time; true solar time gives the hour angle \omega (15° per hour from solar noon); then spherical trigonometry gives the zenith and azimuth. Our implementation agrees with pvlib's implementation of NREL's Solar Position Algorithm to within 0.02° in the project's cross-check tests. The elevation shown is geometric: refraction lifts the Sun's image by about half a degree at the horizon, which matters for sunrise times but hardly at all for energy.

From sunlight to watts on a panel

Sunlight reaches the ground in two ways. The direct beam comes straight from the Sun's disc and casts sharp shadows. Diffuse light has been scattered by air and haze and arrives from the whole sky; it is what lights a shady spot. Weather stations usually measure the total on flat ground, the global horizontal irradiance (GHI).

The beam weakens as the Sun sinks, because it crosses more atmosphere. The air mass counts how many atmospheres' worth: 1 with the Sun overhead, 2 at 30° elevation, about 38 at the horizon. A tilted panel then collects three things, its plane-of-array irradiance: the beam, reduced by the cosine of the angle of incidence; the part of the sky it can see; and a little light reflected from the ground in front of it, which depends on the ground's albedo. Try fresh snow in front of a steep panel and watch the ground slice of the bar grow.

Add up a day of plane-of-array irradiance and you get the day's insolation in kWh/m². Because standard test conditions use 1000 W/m², that number is also the day's peak sun hours: the hours of full one-sun light that would deliver the same energy.

    Why the best tilt is not quite your latitude

    A common rule of thumb says a fixed panel should be tilted at your latitude. The reasoning is neat: at the equinoxes the noon Sun is then exactly face on, and the panel splits the difference between the high summer Sun and the low winter one. The map above usually disagrees by a few degrees, and the reasons are worth knowing.

    • Summer days are longer. More hours of daylight, with the Sun higher, put more of the year's energy into the summer half. A slightly flatter panel serves those hours better.
    • The winter beam is weaker. A low Sun shines through more air, so winter's light is thinned before it arrives. Tilting steeply for winter gains less than it seems.
    • A flatter panel sees more sky. Diffuse light comes from the whole dome; a flat panel sees all of it, a vertical one only half.

    For Los Angeles at 34° north the clear-sky model gives a best tilt of about 30°, and the curve is flat near the top: ten degrees either way costs only a couple of percent. Real weather usually pushes the best tilt flatter still. Clouds scatter sunlight across the whole sky, which favours panels that face up, so a study using typical-year weather found optimal tilts of 45° at Calgary but only 34° at a cloudier Dutch site at nearly the same latitude. The same study's fit for the Northern Hemisphere gives about 29° at 34° north.

    Sweep every tilt for a south-facing panel in Los Angeles (34.05° N, taken at sea level), add up the clear-sky insolation over a year, and the maximum comes at 30.1°, about 4° flatter than the latitude.
    The Jacobson–Jadhav fit to optimal tilts from real-weather simulations, \beta_\text{opt} = 1.3793 + \varphi(1.2011 + \varphi(-0.014404 + 0.000080509\,\varphi)), gives 28.8° at the same latitude.

    Two details of the model show up if you look closely. Near the equator the best tilt comes out at 0°, though in practice panels are usually given a small tilt so that rain can run off and wash them. And the Southern Hemisphere's optimum comes out a degree or two steeper than the Northern one at the same latitude: the Northern summer half-year is about a week longer, because Earth moves more slowly near aphelion in July. The model leaves out the matching effect of distance, about ±3.3 % in sunlight over the year, which favours the Southern summer and partly cancels it.

    Clear sky is a ceiling

    Current consensus These numbers assume a cloudless, fairly clean sky every day of the year. No real place has that. Clouds can cut a day's sunlight to a small fraction, haze and dust dim the beam, and mountains or buildings shade the panel early and late. How far a real year falls below the ceiling depends strongly on the climate: much further in cloudy Seattle or London than in the Atacama around Calama. PVEducation makes the same point about its own clear-sky calculator: such numbers are not used for sizing real systems.

    The clear-sky model itself is simple. The Meinel formula uses one fixed atmosphere for the whole planet, diffuse light is a flat 10 % of the beam, and the isotropic sky ignores the bright glow around the Sun and along the horizon. It is a teaching model: it gets the shapes and the ratios right, and puts the totals in the right range.

    To estimate a real system, use measured weather. The National Solar Radiation Database (NSRDB) provides satellite-derived hourly sunlight, split into the same DNI, DHI and GHI shown here, and typical-year files built from it. NREL's free PVWatts calculator (the lab was renamed the National Laboratory of the Rockies in December 2025) runs a typical meteorological year through a fuller model with cell temperature and system losses, and returns the monthly and yearly energy for an address. Open-source libraries such as pvlib let you build the same model chain yourself.

    The equations

    How high is the Sun?

    Three angles decide it: your latitude \varphi, the Sun's declination \delta (where it stands overhead today), and the hour angle \omega, which turns 15° for every hour away from solar noon.

    \cos\theta_z = \sin\varphi\,\sin\delta + \cos\varphi\,\cos\delta\,\cos\omega, \qquad \alpha = 90^\circ - \theta_z
    \theta_z
    solar zenith angle, from straight overhead (°)
    \alpha
    solar elevation above the horizon (°)
    \varphi
    latitude, north positive (°)
    \delta
    solar declination, between about −23.4° and +23.4° (°)
    \omega
    hour angle: 0 at solar noon, 15° per hour, negative in the morning (°)
    Los Angeles at solar noon on the June solstice: \varphi = 34.05°, \delta = 23.44°, \omega = 0. At noon the formula reduces to \theta_z = \varphi - \delta = 10.61°, so the Sun stands 79.4° high.
    On the equator at the Greenwich meridian, on the March 2026 equinox, the full NOAA calculation puts the Sun 89.96° high at 12:07 UTC. It is at its highest, almost exactly overhead, 7 minutes after clock noon because of the equation of time.
    Longyearbyen (78.22° N) on the December solstice: the highest the Sun gets is 90° - |\varphi - \delta| = −11.66°, below the horizon all day. That is polar night.

    How strong is the beam?

    The lower the Sun, the longer its path through the air. For a flat atmosphere the path would grow as 1/\cos\theta_z, which blows up at the horizon; Kasten and Young's formula follows the curved, refracting atmosphere instead. An empirical rule then turns air mass into beam strength.

    AM = \frac{1}{\cos\theta_z + 0.50572\,(96.07995^\circ - \theta_z)^{-1.6364}}
    \text{DNI} = 1353\left[(1 - a h)\,0.7^{AM^{0.678}} + a h\right]
    AM
    relative air mass (no unit); \theta_z in degrees
    \text{DNI}
    clear-sky direct normal irradiance (W/m²)
    1353
    the solar constant used by Meinel and Meinel (W/m²)
    0.7,\ 0.678
    empirical: about 70 % of the beam survives one air mass
    a,\ h
    a = 0.14 per km; h is the site's height above sea level (km)
    With the Sun 30° above the horizon (\theta_z = 60°), the light crosses 1.99 atmospheres; the flat-Earth estimate 1/\cos 60° = 2 is still good here.
    At \theta_z = 48.19°, air mass 1.5 (the standard test spectrum's), the beam at sea level is 1353 \times 0.7^{1.5^{0.678}} = 846 W/m².
    With the Sun overhead, the beam is 947 W/m² at sea level but 1074 W/m² in Mexico City, 2.24 km up: there is less air above it. Set the site elevation to 0 and back to see the difference.

    The angle of incidence

    A beam hitting a surface at an angle spreads over more area, so each square metre gets less: the cosine of the angle between the beam and the panel's normal. It combines the Sun's position with the panel's tilt and direction.

    \cos\theta = \cos\theta_z\cos\beta + \sin\theta_z\sin\beta\cos(\gamma_s - \gamma)
    \theta
    angle of incidence between the beam and the panel normal (°)
    \beta
    panel tilt from horizontal (°)
    \gamma_s,\ \gamma
    azimuth of the Sun and of the direction the panel faces, clockwise from north (°)
    A panel tilted 30° facing south (180°), with the Sun 50° high in the south-southwest (zenith 40°, azimuth 200°): \cos\theta = \cos 40°\cos 30° + \sin 40°\sin 30°\cos 20° = 0.965, an angle of incidence of 15°. The panel catches 96.5 % of what a Sun-facing panel would.

    Light on the panel: beam + sky + ground

    The beam arrives through the cosine. The isotropic sky model assumes the diffuse sky is equally bright everywhere, so the panel gets the share of the sky it can see, (1+\cos\beta)/2; the rest of its view is ground, reflecting the albedo's share of the global light.

    G_\text{POA} = \text{DNI}\,\max(\cos\theta, 0) + \text{DHI}\,\frac{1 + \cos\beta}{2} + \text{GHI}\,\rho\,\frac{1 - \cos\beta}{2}
    G_\text{POA}
    plane-of-array irradiance (W/m²)
    \text{DHI},\ \text{GHI}
    diffuse and global irradiance on horizontal ground (W/m²); here DHI = 0.1 DNI and GHI = DNI cos θz + DHI
    \rho
    ground albedo (no unit)
    The same panel and Sun, with DNI = 800 W/m², DHI = 80 W/m², GHI = 692.8 W/m² over grass (ρ = 0.2): beam 772.3 + sky 74.6 + ground 9.3 = 856 W/m², about 24 % more than flat ground gets.

    Liu and Jordan's 1963 tilted-surface method made this model standard, though a historical review traces correct isotropic results back to 1942, and recommends anisotropic models for accurate work. Real skies are brighter near the Sun (circumsolar light) and along the horizon, so the isotropic model tends to underestimate light on Sun-facing tilted panels. Models such as Hay–Davies and Perez, available in pvlib, split the diffuse light into these parts.

    From sunlight to energy

    A panel's rating is the power it makes at 1000 W/m². So each peak sun hour is worth one hour at rated power, and the performance ratio takes off the losses.

    H = \int G_\text{POA}\,dt, \qquad E = P_\text{STC} \times \text{PSH} \times PR
    H
    insolation over a day (kWh/m²); the page sums 10-minute steps
    \text{PSH}
    peak sun hours: numerically equal to H in kWh/m² (h)
    P_\text{STC}
    array rating at standard test conditions (kWp)
    PR
    performance ratio, typically 0.75–0.85 (no unit)
    E
    energy delivered (kWh)
    Flat ground in Los Angeles on a clear June solstice collects 8.56 kWh/m²: 8.56 peak sun hours, spread over more than 14 hours of daylight.
    A 5 kWp array on a day with 5.5 peak sun hours and a performance ratio of 0.8: 5 \times 5.5 \times 0.8 = 22 kWh.
    The same array tilted 30° south in Los Angeles, with the clear-sky year's 2443 kWh/m² at sea level (the widget adds the city's 90 m of height): 5 \times 2443 \times 0.8 = 9772 kWh per year, a ceiling that real weather will not reach.
