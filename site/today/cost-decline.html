Solar today

The cost collapse

In 1975 one watt of solar panel cost about $132 in today's money. In 2024 it cost about 27 cents. Few things people make have ever become five hundred times cheaper.

The price on this page is the average selling price of a crystalline-silicon module, per watt of peak power, corrected for inflation to 2025 US dollars. A module is only part of what a solar plant costs. Inverters, racks, wiring, land and labour are the rest, and further down the page we look at the cost of the electricity itself.

  1. 1975 Oil shock money. After the 1973–74 oil embargo, governments began paying for solar research and the first ground-based markets. Panels still cost over $100 per watt, so buyers were mostly satellites and remote equipment.
  2. 2000 Germany's Renewable Energy Sources Act guaranteed a fixed price for 20 years for solar power fed into the grid, creating a large, reliable market.
  3. 2008–2012 The steepest fall in the record: from $4.75 to $1.11 per watt in four years, more than three-quarters off. Studies of the long decline find that after about 2001, economies of scale in ever-larger factories became a bigger driver of cost cuts than research.
  4. 2011 on Manufacturing moved to China. China invested over $50 billion in new solar factories from 2011, and by 2022 held over 80 % of every manufacturing step, from purified silicon to finished panels.
The prices as a table
From $132.38 per watt in 1975 to $0.2652 in 2024 is a fall of 99.8 %: today's panel costs one five-hundredth of what it did. A 400-watt panel would have cost about $53 000 in 1975, in today's money.

Why it fell: learning by doing

In 1936 the aircraft engineer Theodore Wright noticed a pattern in aircraft factories: each time the total number of planes built doubled, the labour to build one fell by a steady share. The pattern is now called Wright's law, and the share is the learning rate. For solar modules it is known as Swanson's law, after the founder of the solar company SunPower, Richard Swanson.

The doubling is of the total ever made, the cumulative capacity, not of time. Solar got cheap fast because the world kept doubling how much it had built: from about 1.2 gigawatts in 2000 to about 2 400 gigawatts by the end of 2025. 2026-09-30

Current consensus No single invention did it. Studies of the decline point to many causes adding up: more efficient cells, thinner wafers and less silicon per watt, much bigger factories, and steady research. Public research money mattered most early on; markets created by policy, as in Germany, Japan and later China, paid for the scale.

Wright's law

If every doubling of the total ever built takes off the same share of the cost, then after n doublings the cost has been multiplied by that remainder n times.

C_n = C_0\,(1 - LR)^{\,n}, \qquad n = \log_2\!\frac{Q}{Q_0}
C_0,\ C_n
cost at the start and after n doublings, US$ per watt
LR
learning rate: the fraction of cost removed per doubling (0.2 means 20 %)
Q_0,\ Q
cumulative capacity at the start and now, gigawatts (GW)
n
number of doublings, a plain number
With a 20 % learning rate, ten doublings (a 1 024-fold increase in everything ever built) leave 0.8^{10} = 0.107 of the starting cost: about nine-tenths off.
The world had 41.5 GW of solar at the end of 2010 and 1 883 GW at the end of 2024. That is \log_2(1883 / 41.5) = 5.50 doublings in fourteen years.
Over those 5.50 doublings the module price fell from $2.51 to $0.265 per watt. The learning rate that fits both ends is 1 - 2^{\ln(0.2652/2.5118)/\ln(1883/41.5)} = 33.5 % per doubling, faster than the long-run average.

A least-squares straight line through all 25 points from 2000 to 2024 on log–log axes gives a learning rate of 27.7 % per doubling. Fraunhofer ISE's fit over the past 45 years gives about 26.6 % (Photovoltaics Report, 14 July 2026; its executive summary rounds this to 26.7 %).

Fitting caveats. The price series joins several sources (Nemet to 2003, Farmer and Lafond for 2004–2009, then IRENA's benchmark prices for modules sold in Europe, with OWID's inflation adjustment), and prices are not costs: in boom years margins swell, in gluts manufacturers sell below cost. A log-log fit is dominated by where the points are dense, so the fitted rate depends on the window: 2000–2024 gives about 28 %, 2010–2024 end to end about 34 %. The x axis here is installed capacity, a proxy for cumulative production (shipments run slightly ahead of installations). Farmer and Lafond, forecasting costs for 53 technologies with a Moore's-law-style time trend, note that an earlier test on very similar data found Wright's law and time trends roughly tied; the two agree whenever production grows exponentially.

The price of solar electricity

What a household or grid pays for is electricity, not panels. The levelised cost of electricity spreads everything a plant costs over all the energy it makes in its life. For new large solar farms worldwide it fell from about 41 cents per kilowatt-hour in 2010 to about 4.3 cents in 2024, in 2025 dollars. 2026-09-30

From $0.4077 to $0.043 per kWh is a fall of 89.5 % in fourteen years. The fall flattened after 2023: the 2025 figure, $0.0435, is no lower.

Cheaper panels are only part of that. Plants also got bigger, sun-tracking racks and two-sided modules squeezed out more energy per panel, and finance got cheaper as investors learned the risks were low. You can try the LCOE formula yourself in the comparison dashboard.
