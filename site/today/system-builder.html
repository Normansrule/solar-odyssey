    The four design rules

    Real installers juggle these same limits. Try breaking each one on purpose.

    1. Never exceed the inverter's maximum voltage, even on the coldest morning

    Silicon's voltage rises as it cools. A string that is comfortably under the limit at noon in July can exceed it at sunrise in January, when the modules are cold and no current is flowing yet. That can destroy an inverter, so codes require the open-circuit voltage to be corrected to the lowest expected temperature. The builder uses the open-circuit voltage coefficient of its own cell model, about −0.27 % per °C, which is close to what datasheets for silicon modules show.

    2. Stay inside the tracking window

    An inverter can only track the maximum power point over a range of voltages, its MPPT window. On a hot afternoon the string's working voltage drops; if it falls below the window, the inverter cannot hold the best point and power is lost. The builder checks the working voltage with cells at 70 °C.

    3. Respect the current limit

    Strings in parallel add their currents. Inverter inputs have a maximum short-circuit current they can safely take. The builder adds a 25 % safety margin to the array's short-circuit current, for moments when sunlight is brighter than the rating conditions.

    4. Choose the DC/AC ratio, and accept a little clipping

    It sounds wasteful to fit more panel power than the inverter can pass, but it usually pays. Panels deliver their full rated power only on rare cool, bright moments; most of the day they make far less. An inverter a bit smaller than the array is cheaper and works nearer its rating for more hours. The price is clipping around noon on the sunniest days, when output is capped at the inverter's rating. The DC/AC ratio measures the trade.

    \text{DC/AC} = \frac{P_\text{array, STC}}{P_\text{inverter, AC}}, \qquad P_\text{AC}(t) = \min\bigl(\eta\,P_\text{DC}(t),\ P_\text{inverter}\bigr)
    P_\text{array, STC}
    array rating at standard test conditions (kWp)
    P_\text{inverter}
    inverter's AC rating (kW)
    P_\text{DC}(t)
    power the array delivers at time t (kW)
    \eta
    inverter conversion efficiency (the builder assumes 0.97)
    Twelve 548 W modules make a 6.58 kWp array. On a 5 kW inverter the DC/AC ratio is 6.58 ÷ 5 = 1.32.
    If a day's power rose and fell like half a sine wave peaking at 10 kW over 12 hours, an 8 kW inverter would clip off only 8.5 % of the day's energy, although it is 20 % smaller than the peak. The flat top is a thin slice of the day.

    For the half-sine day P(t) = P_\text{pk}\sin(\pi t/D) with cap P_\text{ac} < P_\text{pk}, the day's energy is 2P_\text{pk}D/\pi and the clipped energy is \tfrac{2D}{\pi}\bigl(P_\text{pk}\cos\theta - P_\text{ac}(\tfrac{\pi}{2}-\theta)\bigr) with \theta = \arcsin(P_\text{ac}/P_\text{pk}). The builder does not assume a sine: it integrates the clear-sky plane-of-array trace in 10-minute steps, with cell temperature from the NOCT model and the power coefficient of the diode model, and its unit tests check the numerical integral against this closed form.

    What the builder leaves out

    It is a teaching model, not design software. The sky is always clear, so the daily and yearly energies are upper bounds; real weather lowers them, by how much depends on the place. Wiring, dirt and mismatch losses are lumped into a flat 10 %, and the inverter converts at a flat 97 %. The inverters are illustrative, not real products, though their limits are typical of string inverters on the market. Real projects use tools such as pvlib that model weather data, shading and component datasheets in detail. The from cell to grid lesson explains the physics behind each rule.
