Efficiency over time

In 1954 the best solar cell turned about one-sixteenth of the sunlight on it into electricity. The best cells today turn more than a third of ordinary sunlight into electricity, and stacked cells under concentrated light nearly half. Here is how each kind of cell climbed, record by record.

Efficiency is the share of the sunlight's power that comes out as electricity. The records on this page are for research cells: small, carefully made laboratory devices, often smaller than a fingernail, built to find out how far a technology can go. A record only counts once an independent lab has measured it under the same standard light; that is a certified efficiency.

What the lines say

Silicon climbed slowly, then flattened. Settled history Bell Labs' first cell reached about 6 % in 1954. Better surfaces and contacts took it to 22.8 % at the University of New South Wales in 1989, and putting every contact on the back pushed past 26 % in 2017. The record now stands at 28.29 % (LONGi, certified by the ISFH institute in Germany, September 2026). 2026-09-30 That is close to the roughly 29.4 % that silicon can reach at best, once its own unavoidable losses are counted.

Perovskites shot up. Active research The first perovskite cell, in 2009, managed 3.8 % and did not last long. Within four years cells passed 15 %, and in 2026 a 0.05 cm² cell from Hainan University entered the efficiency tables at 28.0 %, after the company SolaEon had announced 27.87 % in January. 2026-10-02 Silicon needed about seventy years to cover the same ground. Lasting outdoors for decades is the open problem; see the frontier.

Stacks win. Every line above the dotted single-junction limit is a stack of two or more cells. A perovskite layer on silicon reached 35.5 % in 2026, and a four-cell stack of III-V semiconductors under 665 suns of concentrated light holds the overall record of 47.6 %, set by Fraunhofer ISE in 2022. 2026-09-30

The ceiling for one junction

A cell with a single band gap wastes photons below the gap and turns the extra energy of the others into heat. The best it can do in ordinary sunlight is the Shockley–Queisser limit: about 33 %, reached at a gap near 1.34 eV. Silicon's gap (1.12 eV) is a little narrower than the ideal, and its indirect gap adds extra losses, which is why its own ceiling is lower.

Rühle's tabulation gives 33.16 % at 1.34 eV for the AM1.5G reference spectrum at 25 °C; figures of up to about 33.7 % that you will often see come from calculations with slightly different spectra and assumptions. The chart draws Rühle's value. The silicon figure of 29.4 % adds Auger recombination and free-carrier absorption for an undoped 110 µm wafer.

Records are not what goes on your roof

A record cell is a best case: tiny, hand-made and measured once. A module joins dozens of full-size cells behind glass, with gaps, wiring and a frame that make no power. The average crystalline-silicon module sold in late 2024 was 22.7 % efficient, against 28 % for the best silicon cell; the best certified full-size silicon module reached 26.4 % in 2026. 2026-10-02 Ten years earlier, typical modules were around 17 %. Record cells point the way; factories follow a few years behind.

How a record is set. The cell is sent to an accredited test centre (NREL, Fraunhofer ISE CalLab, ISFH, ESTI, AIST, NPVM and a few others) and measured under standard test conditions: 1000 W/m² of the AM1.5 global spectrum at 25 °C, over a stated area. The Solar cell efficiency tables, published twice a year (in Progress in Photovoltaics until Version 67, in Joule from Version 68), list results above 1 cm² as main entries and smaller devices as notable exceptions; NREL's chart shows the best result for each technology regardless of size.

What we left out. We plot only milestones we could check against the original paper, the certifying lab or the institution's own release. Several families are missing for that reason: multicrystalline and amorphous silicon, cadmium telluride and single-junction gallium arsenide. NREL's chart has all of them. Company announcements (SolaEon 2026, LONGi 2023–2026) are marked as such in the table and may be revised when the next efficiency tables appear.
