Cell technologies today

Almost every solar panel sold today starts as a single crystal of silicon pulled from a melt. What changed in the last decade is what happens on its two surfaces.

A solar cell has one job: let light in, turn as many photons as possible into free electrons and holes, and get those carriers out through metal wires before they find each other again. Every technology on this page is a different answer to the same three questions: what absorbs the light, how are the two kinds of carrier separated, and how do they reach the wires without being lost on the way?

From sand to wafer

Crystalline silicon cells start as quartz. An arc furnace strips the oxygen away with carbon and leaves silicon that is about 98 % pure, nowhere near clean enough for a solar cell. The Siemens process turns it into a liquid, trichlorosilane, distils out the impurities, and deposits pure silicon back onto hot rods over more than a week. The result is polysilicon.

Polysilicon is then melted and made into an ingot in one of two ways.

  • Pulling one crystal (Czochralski). A small seed crystal is dipped into the melt and drawn slowly upward while it turns. Silicon freezes onto it atom by atom in the same orientation, growing a single cylindrical crystal. Jan Czochralski published the method in 1918, having found it while measuring how fast metals crystallise. The result is monocrystalline silicon.
  • Casting a block. The melt is left to freeze in a square crucible. It is quicker and cheaper, but the block freezes into many grains, each a crystal pointing a different way: multicrystalline silicon, the speckled blue panels that were common until a few years ago.

Diamond-coated wire saws then slice the ingot into wafers about 140 µm thick. Thinner wafers, less waste and better cells cut the silicon needed per watt from about 16 grams in 2004 to about 2 grams in 2024.

Why single crystals won

Settled history The edges between grains in multicrystalline silicon are traps where electrons and holes recombine, and they can short-circuit the junction. For decades the lower cost of casting made up for it. What tipped the balance was better cell design: PERC and its successors gain much more on a single-crystal wafer than on a cast one. Monocrystalline silicon became dominant from about 2018, and by 2020 multicrystalline cells were hard to buy. In 2025 single-crystal silicon made about 690 of the roughly 706 gigawatts of modules produced worldwide; multicrystalline has in practice disappeared. 2026-09-30

Czochralski crystals grow inside a quartz crucible, which dissolves slowly and leaves around 1018 oxygen atoms per cm³ in the ingot. In boron-doped (p-type) silicon, boron and oxygen form defects under light that cut carrier lifetime: light-induced degradation. Phosphorus-doped (n-type) wafers do not suffer it, and neither do gallium-doped p-type wafers. That is one reason the industry moved to n-type wafers along with TOPCon and heterojunction cells; ITRPV and Fraunhofer ISE both put the n-type share above 80 % in 2025.

      Four ways to build a silicon cell

      All four modern silicon designs use the same wafer. They differ in how they treat its surfaces, because that is where most of the remaining losses are. A bare silicon surface is full of broken chemical bonds that swallow carriers. Covering it with a layer that ties those bonds up is called passivation, and better passivation is the story of the last fifteen years.

      PERC (passivated emitter and rear cell) coats the back of the wafer with a passivating film and lets the aluminium contact touch the silicon only through small laser-cut openings. The design was demonstrated at the University of New South Wales in 1989, at 22.8 %, and decades later became the industry's standard cell.

      TOPCon (tunnel oxide passivated contact) goes further: the whole back is covered by an oxide only a nanometre or two thick, topped with doped polysilicon. Carriers tunnel through the oxide to the contact, but the surface stays passivated everywhere, even under the metal. Fraunhofer ISE published the idea in 2014. It overtook PERC in 2024.

      Heterojunction (HJT) cells wrap the crystal wafer on both sides in layers of amorphous silicon a few nanometres thick, which passivate the surfaces extremely well and form the junction. A transparent conductive oxide carries the current sideways to the metal fingers. Their high voltage also makes them lose less power when hot.

      Back-contact cells move every contact to the back, in interleaved positive and negative fingers, so no metal shades the front. A back-contact heterojunction cell passed 26 % in 2017, and back-contact designs hold the silicon record today (see the state of the field). 2026-09-30

      The blue colour is a coating

      Bare polished silicon reflects over 30 % of the light that hits it. Cells fight this twice: the wafer surface is etched into tiny pyramids that bounce reflected light back into the cell, and a transparent anti-reflection coating is laid on top. If the coating is a quarter of a wavelength thick, light reflected from its top and from its bottom cancels out.

      The coating's refractive index should sit halfway (in the geometric sense) between air and silicon, and its thickness should be a quarter of the wavelength measured inside it.

      n_1 = \sqrt{n_0\, n_2}, \qquad d_1 = \frac{\lambda_0}{4\, n_1}
      n_0, n_2
      refractive index of the medium above (air: 1) and of silicon (about 3.5)
      n_1
      refractive index of the coating (no unit)
      \lambda_0
      wavelength in vacuum that should reflect least (nm)
      d_1
      coating thickness (nm)
      Between air (n = 1) and silicon (n = 3.5) the ideal coating index is √3.5 = 1.87.
      To cancel reflection at 600 nm, near the peak of sunlight, the coating is 600 ÷ (4 × 1.87) = 80.2 nm thick: about a thousandth of the wafer's thickness. Blue light is reflected a little, which is why cells look dark blue.

      Thin films

      A thin-film cell is not sliced from a crystal. Its semiconductor is deposited a few micrometres thick straight onto a sheet of glass or foil, which needs far less material and fewer steps. The catch so far has been lower efficiency.

      Cadmium telluride (CdTe) is the thin film that made it to scale. Light enters through the glass, passes a transparent contact and a thin window layer, and is absorbed in the CdTe. Its main maker, First Solar, sells modules of 18 to 20 % efficiency; thin film was about 2 % of world production in 2025. 2026-09-30

      CIGS (copper indium gallium selenide) has a band gap that can be tuned by swapping gallium for indium, and it can be grown on flexible foil. Lab cells reached 19.9 % in 2008, but its production has stayed small next to silicon and CdTe.

      Amorphous silicon was the first thin film, demonstrated at RCA in 1976. It absorbs light strongly but converts it poorly. Today it matters most as the few-nanometre coating inside heterojunction cells.

      A 580 W module measuring 2.28 m × 1.13 m (2.58 m²) has an efficiency of 580 ÷ (1000 × 2.58) = 22.5 %.

      Why does a heterojunction module lose less power when hot? Open-circuit voltage falls with temperature because the diode's saturation current rises steeply. The relative fall is smaller the closer the cell's voltage already sits to the band-gap voltage, so a cell with better passivation (higher Voc) has a smaller coefficient. Measurements on Al-BSF, PERC, PERT and heterojunction cells show this ordering, and also that the series resistance added by stringing cells into a module makes a module's coefficient worse than its cells'.
