From cell to grid

One silicon cell gives you about half a volt. The grid wants hundreds of volts of alternating current, around the clock. Here is everything that sits in between.

Cells into a module

A single silicon cell in full sun produces about 0.7 V with no load (its open-circuit voltage) and about 0.6 V at its best working point, whatever its size. A bigger cell gives more current, not more voltage. To reach useful voltages, cells are wired in series, the front of one to the back of the next, so their voltages add while the same current flows through all of them. Laminated between glass and a back sheet, with a frame and a junction box, that chain is a solar module.

Voltages of cells in series add; currents of chains in parallel add.

V_\text{module} = N_\text{s}\, V_\text{cell}, \qquad I_\text{module} = N_\text{p}\, I_\text{chain}
N_\text{s}
number of cells in series in one chain
N_\text{p}
number of chains in parallel (1 for full cells, 2 for half-cut cells)
V_\text{cell}, I_\text{chain}
voltage of one cell (V), current of one chain (A)
A 144-half-cell module is wired as two chains of 72 half cells. Each chain gives 72 × 0.70 V = 50.4 V open-circuit, the same as a module of 72 full cells.
The site's diode model of that module gives 50.7 V open-circuit and about 13 A short-circuit current, close to a real module of this size.

Why 60, 72, 120 or 144 cells?

The cell count sets the module's voltage and size. For many years modules held 60 or 72 full square cells, which at about 0.6 V each gives a working voltage in the mid-30s or low-40s of volts. Today's modules usually hold 120 or 144 half-cut cells: each full cell is cut in two by a laser, and the halves are wired as two chains in parallel. The voltage stays the same as 60 or 72 full cells, but each chain carries only half the current.

That matters because the power wasted as heat in a wire grows with the square of the current. Halve the current and the loss in the same wire drops to a quarter.

P_\text{loss} = I^2 R
I
current in the wire (A)
R
resistance of the wire (Ω)
Through a ribbon of 0.01 Ω, a full cell's 13.2 A wastes 13.2² × 0.01 = 1.74 W. Half a cell's 6.6 A wastes only 6.6² × 0.01 = 0.436 W, a quarter as much.

Shade and the bypass diode

Series wiring has a weakness. The same current must flow through every cell, so one shaded cell, which can only make a little current, throttles the whole chain like a kink in a hose. Worse, the other cells force it into reverse, so instead of making power it absorbs some and turns it into heat: a hot spot that can crack glass or scorch the back sheet.

The fix is a bypass diode, a one-way valve wired across a group of cells. While the group works normally the diode does nothing. When a cell in the group is shaded and the group's voltage swings negative, the diode starts to conduct and the string current flows around the group instead of through it. The module loses that group's share of power but keeps the rest.

Shaded cells in each group

The model solves each group separately. At the string current I it finds every cell's voltage on its own I-V curve, including reverse bias through the shunt resistance, and adds them. If the group total would fall below minus one diode drop (taken as 0.5 V), the diode conducts, clamping the group there, and the cells carry only the current that leaves their sum at that clamp. Notice that even with a diode the shaded cell still dissipates some heat: it is reverse-biased by the sum of the other 23 cells' voltages. PVEducation suggests no more than about 15 silicon cells per diode to keep a shaded cell safe; the widget's groups of 24 are larger than that, which is why the shaded cell still warms. The model's shunt is idealised and real cells break down in reverse in ways it does not capture.

Modules into strings and arrays

An inverter works best at a few hundred volts or more, far above one module's 40-odd. So modules are wired in series again, into a string. To collect more current, several strings are connected in parallel, all at the same voltage, into an array. The rule is the same as for cells: series adds voltage, parallel adds current.

Three strings of 13.2 A modules in parallel deliver 3 × 13.2 A = 39.6 A at the voltage of one string.

The cold-morning limit

A cell's voltage rises as it gets colder, by about 2 mV for every degree. The highest voltage a string ever reaches is therefore on a bright, freezing morning, before the sun has warmed the modules. Electrical codes ask designers to size strings for that moment: in the United States, the National Electrical Code (NEC) asks for the open-circuit voltage corrected to the lowest expected temperature, using the maker's temperature coefficient.

V_\text{oc,cold} = N \cdot V_\text{oc,STC}\,\bigl(1 + \beta\,(T_\text{min} - 25\,^\circ\text{C})\bigr)
N
modules in series
V_\text{oc,STC}
module open-circuit voltage at 25 °C (V)
\beta
voltage temperature coefficient, as a fraction per °C (negative; −0.27 %/°C is −0.0027 per °C)
T_\text{min}
lowest expected temperature (°C)
Eighteen 50.7 V modules with β = −0.27 %/°C at −10 °C reach 18 × 50.7 × (1 + 0.0027 × 35) = 998.8 V, just inside a 1000 V limit.
A nineteenth module would push the string to about 1054 V, so the longest safe string here is 18 modules.

And the hot afternoon

The opposite happens in summer: hot modules give less voltage and less power. A module's datasheet rating is at 25 °C, but in sunshine its cells run far hotter than the air. A simple estimate uses the nominal operating cell temperature (NOCT), measured at 800 W/m², 20 °C air and a light breeze; most modules sit around 40–45 °C.

T_\text{cell} \approx T_\text{air} + \frac{\text{NOCT} - 20\,^\circ\text{C}}{800\ \text{W/m}^2}\,G
T_\text{air}
air temperature (°C)
G
sunlight on the module (W/m²)
On a 25 °C day in full 1000 W/m² sun, a module with a NOCT of 45 °C runs at about 25 + 25 × 1000 / 800 = 56 °C.
With a power coefficient of −0.30 %/°C, a 550 W module then gives 550 × (1 − 0.003 × 31.25) = 498 W, about 9 % below its rating.

The inverter and maximum power point tracking

Modules make direct current (DC). Homes and the grid run on alternating current (AC), which swings back and forth 50 or 60 times a second. The inverter converts one into the other by switching transistors on and off thousands of times a second and smoothing the result into a sine wave locked to the grid's.

Its second job is just as important. The power a string delivers depends on the voltage the inverter holds it at. Too low and the voltage is wasted; too high and the current collapses. In between lies the maximum power point, and it moves every time a cloud passes or the modules warm up. Following it is called maximum power point tracking (MPPT).

The simplest tracker, perturb and observe, nudges the voltage a little, checks whether the power went up, and keeps going if it did or turns back if it did not. It is cheap and works well on a curve with a single hump. But partial shading, with bypass diodes doing their job, gives the curve two or more humps. A tracker that only looks at its neighbourhood can settle happily on a lower one. That is why trackers also run an occasional global scan across the whole voltage range.

Perturb and observe compares P_k with P_{k-1} after each voltage step \pm\Delta V and reverses when power falls. At steady state it oscillates around the peak by about one step, so a larger step tracks fast changes quickly but wastes more power in steady sun. It can also be fooled when irradiance changes during a step, mistaking a brighter sky for a better voltage; incremental-conductance methods compare dI/dV with -I/V instead. Under partial shading each bypassed group adds a local maximum, roughly at multiples of a group's voltage, which global methods exploit to scan only a few candidate voltages.

Balance of system

Everything except the modules is called the balance of system. Most of it is unglamorous and all of it matters.

  • Mounting. Roof rails and clamps on homes; steel racks driven into the ground on plants. Fixed racks tilt the modules toward the equator.
  • Trackers. On many large plants each row of modules sits on a motorised tracker that turns it east in the morning and west in the afternoon, catching more of the day's light. The sun path lesson shows why.
  • Wiring and connectors. Sunlight-resistant DC cable, matched plug-in connectors, and combiner boxes that join strings.
  • Protection. Fuses for strings in parallel (so one faulty string cannot swallow the current of the others), DC and AC disconnect switches, surge protection and grounding.
  • Monitoring. Meters and data loggers that spot a failed string or a dirty array.

Connecting to the grid, and the rules

A system that feeds the public grid is not just plugged in. The inverter must hold its output within the grid's voltage and frequency limits, ride through brief disturbances, and, above all, stop exporting if the grid goes down, so that it never energises a line a repair crew believes is dead. That safety function is called anti-islanding. Four standards come up again and again.
