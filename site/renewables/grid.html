The smart grid

A power grid is the largest machine people have built, and it has almost no memory. Every second, the power going in must equal the power coming out. Wind and solar make that balancing act harder, and storage, flexible demand, long wires and clever electronics make it possible.

The fast-growing part, wind and solar, is the part that follows the weather. Latest figures: State of the field.

A battery in a torch stores energy; the grid mostly does not. Electricity is made at the same instant it is used, so somebody, the grid operator, has to match the two continuously, choosing which plants run and how hard: dispatch. For a century they did it by adjusting fuel-burning plants. With wind and solar, the fuel is the weather, so the grid needs other ways to keep its balance.

From a cable to Gotland to grid-forming inverters

  1. 1954

    Direct current under the sea. The first commercial high-voltage direct current link, 100 kV and 20 MW, connects the island of Gotland to mainland Sweden through a 96 km undersea cable.

  2. 2015

    A field guide to the duck. NREL analyses California's "duck chart" and warns that, without more flexibility, solar at about 20 % of annual energy could see over 30 % of extra output curtailed.

  3. 2016

    13,000 MW in three hours. California's grid operator explains that by 2020 it expects to need about 13,000 MW of extra supply within roughly three hours as the Sun sets on spring days.

  4. 2017

    The big battery. South Australia's Hornsdale Power Reserve, 100 MW / 129 MWh, opens as the world's largest lithium-ion battery. The same year NREL engineers set out what it would take to run a grid on 100 % renewable power.

  5. 2019

    1,100 kV across China. The Changji–Guquan direct-current link, at 1,100 kV, carries 12 GW over more than 3,000 km.

  6. 2020

    A roadmap for grid-forming inverters. US researchers set out how inverters could hold up a grid's voltage and frequency themselves, a job spinning generators have always done. Active research

  7. 2023

    Batteries flatten the duck. California's battery fleet has grown from 0.2 GW in 2018 to 4.9 GW, storing midday solar for the evening.

Why balance every second: frequency and inertia

The whole grid's alternating current swings back and forth together, 50 times a second in most of the world and 60 in the Americas: the grid frequency. It is the grid's speedometer. If a power station suddenly trips off, demand exceeds supply, and the missing energy is taken from the spinning turbines and generators, which slow down a little; the frequency falls. That spinning mass is the grid's inertia: like a heavy flywheel, it cannot be stopped quickly, which buys the few seconds needed for reserves to respond.

Solar panels and most wind turbines connect through inverters, power electronics with no spinning mass coupled to the grid. As they replace coal and gas plants, the grid has less inertia, and the frequency falls faster after a fault. Current consensus

The rate of change of frequency

In the first instant after a plant trips, nothing has responded yet; the only thing that slows the fall is the stored spinning energy. Lose twice the power and the frequency falls twice as fast; have twice the inertia and it falls half as fast.

A 50 Hz grid with 50,000 MVA of spinning machines and an assumed H of 4 s loses a 1,000 MW power station: 1,000 × 50 ÷ (2 × 4 × 50,000) = 0.125 Hz/s, falling. Halve the inertia (H = 2 s) and it falls at 0.25 Hz/s.

Equipment is designed to ride through a certain RoCoF; grid codes ask generators to tolerate from about 1 Hz/s (Ireland, Great Britain) to 2 Hz/s (continental Europe) and, in Australia's preferred standard, 4 Hz/s over a quarter of a second.

Model: a single swing equation for the whole grid, reserves ramping linearly to the lost power, and a little load damping (demand from motors falls slightly as frequency falls). Real grids add governor dynamics, under-frequency load shedding and network effects.

Notice how speed can stand in for inertia: with H at just 1 s, reserves that take over within a second hold the dip to about 49.8 Hz, while reserves that need 20 seconds let it fall to about 49.2 Hz, deeper than the heavy grid with H = 4 s ever goes. This is why grids with a lot of solar and wind buy fast frequency response from batteries, and why researchers are developing grid-forming inverters that behave like synthetic inertia.

The duck curve

Plot a spring day's demand minus the output of solar and wind, the net load that every other source must supply, and something odd appears as solar grows. The middle of the day sags into a deep belly while the evening still peaks, so the curve looks like a duck. California's grid operator named the shape; the belly brings curtailment, and the neck brings a steep evening climb of about 13,000 MW in three hours that the rest of the system must meet. Batteries, demand response and better forecasting are the main remedies.

The toolbox of a smart grid

Transmission and HVDC

Weather is local; a continent is not. Long lines smooth out local calm and cloud and carry power from where it is made to where it is used. High-voltage direct current lines lose roughly 3.5 % per 1,000 km, about half what an AC line at the same voltage would, plus under 1 % in the converter stations, and can link grids that are not synchronised. Interconnectors between countries let each import when short and export when in surplus.

Demand response

Instead of moving supply to match demand, move demand to match supply. Demand response means heating water, charging cars or running industrial processes at midday when solar is plentiful, and easing off at the evening peak, often in exchange for a lower price.

Forecasting

A surprise is far more expensive than a forecast. Weather models and machine learning now predict wind and solar output hours to days ahead; a US Department of Energy project reported a 30 % improvement in solar forecast accuracy.

Sensors and automation

A smart grid measures what is happening across the network in near real time and acts on it automatically: rooftop inverters that ride through faults, batteries that respond in a second, controllers that reroute power around a fault.

What works today, and what is still hard

Working today

  • Wind and solar already make about a sixth of the world's electricity, and far more in some countries (compare Denmark or Spain on the overview chart).
  • Batteries now provide fast frequency response and shift solar into the evening.
  • Long HVDC lines move gigawatts over thousands of kilometres.

Still hard

  • Weeks of calm, cloudy winter weather need long-duration or seasonal storage, or firm low-carbon plants. Active research
  • Stability with very little spinning inertia depends on grid-forming inverters still being proven at scale.
  • New transmission lines take many years to plan, permit and build.
