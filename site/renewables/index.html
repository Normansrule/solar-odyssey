Solar Odyssey

The clean-energy system

A solar panel on its own is not an energy system. Sunshine, wind and falling water arrive when the weather says so; people want power when their day says so. Between the two sit wires, stores and some clever timing. This page is the map.

33.8 %

of the world's electricity came from renewable sources in 2025: 10,727 TWh from hydro, wind, solar, bioenergy, geothermal and the sea. 2026-09-30

The rest came from coal, gas, oil and nuclear power. The latest figures are collected on the State of the field page.

Every power system does the same three jobs. It makes electricity, it moves it, often hundreds of kilometres, and it matches supply to demand, second by second, because a grid holds almost no energy of its own. Coal and gas plants made the matching easy: burn more fuel when people switch on more lights. Variable renewables such as wind and solar make the first job cheap and clean, but they hand the third job to new players: storage, flexible demand and a better-connected grid.

The smart grid lesson

The map as a list
  • Solar photovoltaics. Cells turn light straight into electricity, with no moving parts. Output follows the Sun: zero at night, highest around noon, lower in winter and under cloud.
  • Concentrated solar power. Mirrors focus sunlight to heat molten salt or oil, which drives a steam turbine. Because heat is easy to store, the turbine can keep running after sunset. In this dataset its output is counted inside solar, and it is a small part of it.
  • Wind. Turbines catch the kinetic energy of moving air. Output follows the weather and often peaks at night or in winter, which complements solar.
  • Hydropower. Water falling through turbines. Dams can hold water back and release it on demand, which makes hydro the largest and most flexible renewable source.
  • Geothermal. Heat from deep underground drives turbines around the clock. The dataset groups it with tidal and wave power as other renewables.
  • Ocean energy. Tides and waves. Tides are predictable years ahead; today only a handful of plants run. Counted with geothermal in this dataset.
  • Biomass. Wood, crop waste, biogas and other plant matter burned or digested for power. It can be run when needed, like a fuel, but its climate benefit depends on where the biomass comes from.
  • Fossil and nuclear plants today. Coal, gas and oil plants and nuclear reactors still make most of the world's electricity. Shown in grey so the renewable lines can be read against them.
  • The grid. High-voltage transmission lines carry power long distances; local distribution lines bring it to streets and buildings. Operators keep supply and demand equal every second, which shows up as a steady frequency of 50 or 60 hertz.
  • Batteries. Grid batteries, mostly lithium-ion, respond in under a second and typically store a few hours of their full output: ideal for moving midday solar into the evening.
  • Pumped hydro. Water pumped uphill when power is plentiful and released through turbines when it is needed. By far the largest store of electricity in the world today.
  • Hydrogen. Electricity splits water into hydrogen, which can be stored for weeks or months and turned back into power, at the cost of losing well over half the energy on the way.
  • Demand response. Shifting when electricity is used: charging cars and heating water at midday, trimming industrial loads at the evening peak.
  • Interconnectors. Long lines and cables, often high-voltage direct current, that link regions and countries so a calm, cloudy place can import from a windy or sunny one.
  • Homes and buildings. Lighting, appliances, cooling and, more and more, heat pumps. Demand usually peaks in the early evening.
  • Industry. Motors, furnaces, electrolysis and data centres. Some industrial loads can move in time for a price, which makes them a useful source of flexibility.
  • Transport. Electric trains and, increasingly, electric cars. A parked car's battery can charge when power is plentiful.
  • Heat. Heat pumps and electric boilers turn electricity into warmth for buildings and factories. Hot water and heat stores are cheap ways to store energy.

Three ideas make the map work. First, variety: wind often blows when the Sun is down, and a wide area averages out local weather. Second, time-shifting: storage and flexible demand move energy from hours of plenty to hours of need. Third, reach: long transmission lines and interconnectors move energy from where it is plentiful to where it is needed. None of these is new; what is new is how much of each a grid built on wind and solar needs. Current consensus

    Reading the world chart from the top down tells the story of the last fifteen years. Hydropower was the only large renewable source for most of the period; it still makes about 4,400 TWh a year. Wind and solar were a sliver in 2010, about 1.8 % of world electricity together; by 2025 they made 17 %, and in 2025 solar's output passed wind's for the first time in this dataset. Coal is still the single largest source. Choose Denmark, Spain or China to see how differently countries have travelled, and Norway or Brazil to see grids built on hydropower decades ago.

    How to read this section

    The renewable lessons share one layout, so you can compare them: a share of world electricity, a short dated timeline, an animation of how the source works, the key equations (each with a worked example that this site's test suite recomputes), strengths and limits, and references. A suggested route:

    1. Wind, hydropower, geothermal and tidal and wave

      Moving air, falling water, the Earth's heat and the tides: four ways to spin a generator.

    2. Biomass and concentrated solar power

      Two more routes from sunlight: through plants, and through mirrors and heat. Both can store energy before turning it into electricity.

    3. Energy storage

      Batteries, pumped hydro and hydrogen. Size a battery to carry a sunny day into the evening.

    4. The smart grid

      Frequency, inertia, the duck curve and a balancing game over a whole day.

    5. Comparison dashboard

      Capacity factor, land use, cost and growth side by side.

    Terms are underlined with dots: point at one, or tab to it, for a definition. Switch to Expert in the header for derivations and technical detail. Numbers that change from year to year carry a date and are gathered on the State of the field page.

    Grid engineers describe the matching job with a few terms worth knowing before you go on. Net load is demand minus wind and solar: the part everything else must supply. Dispatch is the minute-by-minute choice of which plants and stores run. Curtailment is wind or solar output thrown away because nothing can use or store it. The deeper argument of this section is that, as variable renewables pass a few tens of percent of annual energy, the cost of the system depends less on the cost of each kilowatt-hour generated and more on how well these three are managed.
