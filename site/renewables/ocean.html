Tidal and wave

The sea moves in two great ways. Tides are pulled by the Moon and Sun and arrive on a timetable you can print years ahead. Waves are wind energy gathered over thousands of kilometres of ocean. Both carry enormous power, and both have proved hard and costly to harvest.

< 0.3 %

Tidal and wave power are too small to appear on their own in world electricity statistics. They are counted with geothermal, and all three together made 0.28 % of the world's electricity in 2025 (89 TWh), almost all of it geothermal. 2026-09-30

Installed at the end of 2025: about 0.5 GW of marine energy, against 16 GW of geothermal and 1,291 GW of wind.

Ocean energy is a story of a few landmark plants and many prototypes. Two tidal barrages, in France and South Korea, make most of it; the rest comes from small arrays of tidal-stream turbines and a handful of wave devices. The resource is predictable and plentiful, but the sea is a brutal place for machinery, and costs remain well above those of wind and solar. Current consensus

Six landmarks

  1. 1966

    La Rance, France. A 240 MW barrage across the Rance estuary in Brittany, with 24 bulb turbines that generate in both directions. It still makes roughly 500–600 GWh a year.

  2. 2008

    Aguçadoura, Portugal. The first wave farm: three snake-like Pelamis machines, 2.25 MW in all. Technical problems sent them back to port within months, and the project closed.

  3. 2011

    Mutriku, Spain. A wave plant built into a harbour breakwater: 16 chambers where waves pump air in and out through air turbines, 296 kW as designed (14 turbines, 259 kW, in regular use). It passed 3 GWh in total in 2023.

  4. 2011

    Sihwa Lake, South Korea. Ten 25.4 MW turbines make the 254 MW Sihwa plant the largest tidal power station, at about 550 GWh a year. It generates on the incoming tide and helped clean up a polluted lake.

  5. 2018

    MeyGen, Scotland. Four 1.5 MW tidal-stream turbines in the Pentland Firth enter commercial operation, one of the first tidal-stream arrays; they have since delivered more than 50 GWh.

  6. 2025

    Still small. About 0.5 GW of marine energy is installed worldwide, mostly the two big barrages. 2026-09-30

Tidal barrages

A tidal barrage is a low dam across a bay or estuary. It works like a hydro plant whose reservoir is refilled by the sea twice a day. The simplest scheme is ebb generation: let the rising tide in through sluices, shut the gates at high water, wait while the sea falls away outside, then run the trapped water out through turbines across the height difference. The tidal range is everything: a barrage needs several metres, which only a few coasts have.

The energy in a full basin

When a basin of area A drains through a range R, the water's centre of mass falls by R/2. So the most energy one emptying could give is the weight of the water, ρgAR, times R/2. Range enters squared: double the range and you get four times the energy.

E_{\max} = \tfrac12\,\rho\,g\,A\,R^{2}
E_{\max}
energy released by one full emptying, joules
\rho
density of seawater, about 1,025 kg/m³
A
basin area, square metres
R
tidal range, metres
La Rance's 22 km² basin with its 8.2 m mean range holds at most ½ × 1,025 × 9.81 × 22 × 10⁶ × 8.2² = 7.4 × 10¹² J, about 2.1 GWh per tide. Its real output, roughly 500–600 GWh a year over about 705 tides, is 0.7–0.85 GWh per tide: some 35–40 % of this ideal, since the turbines can only run while there is enough head.

The model above is a time-step simulation: the sea follows a cosine of period 12.42 h (the main lunar tide), flow through sluices and turbines follows the orifice law Q = CdA√(2gh), and electrical power is ηρgQh with η = 0.85. Turbine and sluice areas are chosen so the peak output is close to La Rance's 240 MW. Real plants squeeze out more by generating on both ebb and flood (as La Rance does), by pumping extra water into the basin near high tide when the head is small, and by timing generation to prices. Sihwa generates only on the flood, letting the sea run into the basin.

Tidal stream: a wind turbine under water

Where the tide squeezes through a narrow channel, the water can run at several metres per second. A tidal-stream turbine stands on the sea floor in that current and works exactly like a wind turbine, with the same physics, P = ½ρAv³Cp, and the same Betz limit (see wind). The difference is density: seawater is about 840 times denser than air, so a slow current carries as much power per square metre as a gale.

Wave energy

Wind blowing over the ocean builds waves, and waves carry that energy for thousands of kilometres with little loss. Engineers describe a real, messy sea with two numbers: the significant wave height Hs and the energy period Te. From them comes the wave energy flux, the power arriving across each metre of wave crest.

Wave energy flux

A wave's energy per square metre of sea grows with the square of its height. That energy travels at the wave group's speed, which grows with the period. Multiply the two and you get the power per metre of crest: doubling the wave height quadruples it.

P = \frac{\rho\,g^{2}\,H_s^{2}\,T_e}{64\pi}
P
power per metre of wave crest, watts per metre (W/m)
H_s
significant wave height, metres
T_e
energy period, seconds
\rho,\ g
seawater density (1,025 kg/m³) and gravity (9.81 m/s²)
A moderate sea with Hs = 2 m and Te = 8 s carries 15.7 kW across every metre of crest. A 1 km stretch of such sea brings in about 16 MW.

In deep water the energy per unit area of an irregular sea is E = ρgHs²/16 and it travels at the group velocity cg = gT/(4π), half the phase speed; their product gives the formula above. It holds where the depth exceeds about half a wavelength; in shallow water waves slow, steepen and lose energy to the sea bed. Devices absorb power by moving out of phase with the wave so that they radiate a wave that cancels part of the incoming one; a heaving axisymmetric body can at best absorb the power arriving across a crest width of λ/2π.

Many designs have been tried: floating snakes that flex at their joints, buoys that bob against a sea-bed anchor, flaps hinged at the sea floor, and oscillating water columns like Mutriku, where waves pump air through a turbine. None has yet settled into a standard design the way three-bladed wind turbines did. Active research

Strengths, limits and where ocean energy fits

Strengths

  • Tides are predictable years ahead, so tidal output can be planned exactly.
  • Water's density packs a lot of power into a small, mostly hidden machine.
  • A large untapped resource.

Limits

  • Tiny today: about 0.5 GW installed, against more than 1,000 GW each of wind and hydro.
  • Expensive: few machines are built, each is nearly custom, and installing and maintaining them at sea is costly. Current consensus
  • Storms, corrosion and fouling have ended many projects early, as at Aguçadoura.
  • Barrages change estuaries: tides, sediments and habitats behind the wall.
  • Tides still vary through the day and the fortnightly spring–neap cycle, so output comes in pulses.

For now, ocean energy matters most for remote coasts and islands that pay a lot for imported fuel. Its predictable pulses are easier for a grid to plan around than wind's, but still need storage or other sources between tides. See how it compares with the other sources on the comparison dashboard.
