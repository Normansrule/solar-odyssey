Comparison dashboard

Eleven ways to make electricity, side by side: how many hours they run, what they cost to build and to run, how much land they take, how much greenhouse gas they release over their lives, and how fast they are growing.

No single number says which source is “best”. A solar farm is cheap to build but makes power only when the Sun is up; a geothermal plant costs nearly six times as much per kilowatt but runs almost all year. This page puts the main yardsticks next to each other, each with its unit, its year and the organisation that measured it, so you can see both the gaps and the trade-offs.

Costs come mostly from the International Renewable Energy Agency’s (IRENA) survey of projects finished in 2024, with the Lazard 2025 study of new US plants for nuclear, gas and coal. The two use different methods, so read them as two views, not one ruler. 2026-09-30 Fast-moving numbers are kept current on State of the field.

    What each yardstick means

    Capacity factor
    A plant’s capacity factor is the energy it actually made in a year divided by what it would have made running at full nameplate capacity for all 8,760 hours. It is not an efficiency. For sun and wind it mostly measures how often the resource is there; for coal and gas it also measures how often the plant was asked to run. The US coal fleet ran at only 42.6 % of its capacity in 2024. Current consensus
    Installed cost
    The total installed cost: everything it takes to build the plant, divided by its capacity, in US dollars per kilowatt. A kilowatt of solar is the cheapest to build, but it makes far fewer kilowatt-hours a year than a kilowatt of geothermal, so cost per kilowatt alone misleads.
    Levelized cost of energy
    The levelized cost of energy (LCOE) spreads the building cost, the yearly operations and maintenance (O&M) and the fuel over every kilowatt-hour the plant makes in its life, with interest. It is the price per kilowatt-hour that just pays the plant back. The lab below lets you rebuild it.
    Land use
    Land-use intensity: square metres occupied for each megawatt-hour made per year, counting mines and gas fields as well as the plant. For wind and gas the study gives both the direct footprint and the whole site; the gap is large for wind, because the land between turbines stays farmland.
    Lifecycle emissions
    Lifecycle emissions: greenhouse gases from mining, manufacturing, building, fuel, running and dismantling, in grams of CO2-equivalent (CO2e) per kilowatt-hour, from the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) review of published studies. Current consensus

    Three things change these numbers more than the technology itself. First, the cost of capital: IRENA uses country-specific rates, from 3.8 % in Europe to 12 % in Africa for 2024, so the same wind farm has a much higher LCOE in a country where money is expensive. Second, IRENA computes an LCOE for each project and then averages; the average of many LCOEs is not the LCOE of the average inputs. Third, a fleet average (EIA’s marks) includes old, curtailed and part-time plants, while IRENA and Lazard describe new ones.

    LCOE also ignores when energy arrives. A kilowatt-hour from a dispatchable plant at the evening peak is worth more to a grid than one from a variable source at noon on a sunny day. System studies add storage, backup and transmission costs on top; none of those are in these figures. See The smart grid.

    Equation: capacity factor

    Imagine a plant that never stopped and never dimmed. Its capacity factor is the fraction of that ideal year it actually delivered.

    \mathrm{CF} = \frac{E_\text{year}}{P_\text{rated} \times 8760\ \text{h}}
    \mathrm{CF}
    capacity factor, a fraction between 0 and 1 (often written as a percentage)
    E_\text{year}
    energy delivered in one year, megawatt-hours (MWh)
    P_\text{rated}
    nameplate capacity, megawatts (MW)
    8760 h
    hours in a (non-leap) year
    A 100 MW solar farm that delivers 148,920 MWh in a year has CF = 148,920 ÷ (100 × 8,760) = 0.17, IRENA’s 2024 global average for new solar PV.
    The same farm makes as much energy as 1,489 hours at full power (0.17 × 8,760): its full-load hours. A geothermal plant at 0.88 manages about 7,700.

    Equation: levelized cost of energy

    Think of a mortgage. Building the plant is like buying a house with a loan: you repay it in equal yearly instalments that include interest. Add the yearly running costs, then divide by the energy made each year. That is the LCOE.

    \mathrm{LCOE} = \frac{C \cdot \mathrm{CRF} + F}{8.76 \cdot \mathrm{CF}} + V + \Phi
    \mathrm{CRF} = \frac{r(1+r)^n}{(1+r)^n - 1}
    \mathrm{LCOE}
    levelized cost of energy, US dollars per megawatt-hour ($/MWh); divide by 1,000 for $/kWh
    C
    total installed cost, $/kW
    \mathrm{CRF}
    capital recovery factor: the share of C repaid each year, per year
    r
    real discount rate (cost of capital after inflation), a fraction per year
    n
    economic life, years
    F
    fixed O&M, $/kW per year
    8.76\cdot\mathrm{CF}
    MWh made per kW of capacity per year (8,760 h ÷ 1,000)
    V
    variable O&M, $/MWh
    \Phi
    fuel cost, $/MWh
    Utility solar with IRENA’s 2024 cost, C = 691 $/kW and CF = 0.17, Lazard’s mid-range fixed O&M of 12.5 $/kW-yr and 35-year life, at r = 6 %: \mathrm{CRF} = 0.0690, so capital costs 691 × 0.0690 = 47.7 $/kW-yr. Adding O&M gives 60.2 $/kW-yr, spread over 8.76 × 0.17 = 1.489 MWh: 40.4 $/MWh, about 4.0 US cents per kWh. IRENA’s published average is 4.3 cents.
    Of that, the loan repayment alone is 32.0 $/MWh, four-fifths of the total. Solar and wind are almost all capital, which is why their cost depends so much on the interest rate.

    The annuity form above equals the textbook definition, total discounted costs divided by total discounted energy, \sum_t c_t (1+r)^{-t} \big/ \sum_t e_t (1+r)^{-t}, whenever yearly costs and output are constant. It is the form NREL’s Annual Technology Baseline uses. Discounting the energy is not a trick: it is what makes a constant price per MWh repay the loan.

    Use a real rate with real (inflation-free) costs. Mixing a nominal rate with today’s prices overstates LCOE, most of all for long-lived, capital-heavy plants such as nuclear and hydro.

      LCOE against discount rate

      Assumptions in this lab

      • A real discount rate, constant over the life: costs are in today’s money. Our default of 6 % sits inside IRENA’s 2024 range of 3.8–12 %.
      • No subsidies, tax credits or carbon price.
      • No system or integration costs: no storage, backup, extra transmission or curtailment.
      • Constant output and running costs every year; no degradation; no decommissioning cost; construction counted as one up-front payment.
      • Presets mix sources: IRENA’s global installed cost and capacity factor where they exist, US O&M figures from Lazard or the US Energy Information Administration (EIA). Each input names its source; lifetimes marked “this page” are our assumption.

      With IRENA’s 2024 inputs and these O&M figures, which discount rate reproduces IRENA’s published LCOE exactly? If the answer lands inside IRENA’s 3.8–12 % range, the simple annuity model is consistent with IRENA’s far more detailed project-by-project calculation. The test suite checks this for every technology where it should hold.
