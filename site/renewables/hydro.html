Hydropower

The Sun lifts water as vapour and drops it as rain on high ground. On its way back to the sea, that water can be made to fall through a turbine. Hydropower is the oldest large source of clean electricity and still the biggest.

At the end of 2025 the world had about 1,300 gigawatts of conventional hydropower, plus about 200 gigawatts of pumped storage, which uses the same machines as a giant rechargeable battery. Its share of world electricity has slipped slowly as wind and solar grow faster, but no other renewable yet makes more. The latest figures are on the State of the field page.

From a country house to a 22-gigawatt dam

  1. 1878

    Cragside. William Armstrong's house in Northumberland, England, lights arc lamps from a water-driven dynamo, and in 1880 adds Swan's new incandescent bulbs: the first home lit by hydroelectricity.

  2. 1882

    Appleton, Wisconsin. The Vulcan Street plant, about 12.5 kW, becomes the first Edison hydroelectric central station selling power to customers in North America, weeks after Edison's coal-fired Pearl Street station.

  3. 1895

    Niagara Falls. The Adams plant starts large-scale alternating-current generation; by 1900 ten 5,000-horsepower generators run there, and AC wins the argument over long-distance transmission.

  4. 1936

    Hoover Dam. The first power reaches Los Angeles on 9 October 1936. Today its 17 main units total about 2,080 MW.

  5. 1984

    Itaipu. Brazil and Paraguay's dam on the Paraná starts generating; its twenty 700 MW units total 14,000 MW, and in 2016 it set its own record of 103 TWh in one year.

  6. 2012

    Three Gorges. China's 22,500 MW dam on the Yangtze becomes the world's largest power station. Seasonal swings in the river mean its yearly output is similar to Itaipu's.

  7. 2025

    Water batteries. Pumped storage passes 200 GW worldwide after a record 11.7 GW is added in a single year. 2026-09-30

How a hydro plant works

A dam holds back a river and raises its level. Water enters an intake near the bottom of the reservoir, rushes down a steel pipe called a penstock, and spins a turbine. The turbine turns a generator above it on the same shaft. The spent water leaves through the tailrace back into the river. Two numbers set the power: the head, how far the water falls, and the flow, how much water passes each second.

The hydropower equation

Power from falling water

Every kilogram of water that falls a height H gives up energy gH. Multiply by the kilograms arriving each second, ρQ, and by the share the turbine and generator keep, η. That is all: double the head or double the flow and the power doubles. A modern plant keeps about 90 % of the water's energy, far more than any fuel-burning plant, which is limited by heat-engine physics.

P = \eta\,\rho\,g\,Q\,H
P
electrical power, watts (W)
\eta
efficiency of turbine and generator together, about 0.9 (no units)
\rho
density of water, 1,000 kg/m³
g
gravitational acceleration, 9.81 m/s²
Q
flow through the turbines, cubic metres per second
H
net head, metres
A dam with a 100 m head passing 200 m³/s (a small river's worth) at 90 % efficiency makes 0.9 × 1,000 × 9.81 × 200 × 100 = 176.5 MW.
Water falling 500 m to a Pelton wheel leaves the nozzle at up to √(2 × 9.81 × 500) = 99 m/s, about 360 km/h. That is why high-head plants use jets and cups rather than a propeller.

The net head is the gross drop between reservoir and tailwater minus friction losses in the intake and penstock, which grow with the square of the water speed; designers trade a wider, costlier penstock against lost head. Turbine families are chosen by specific speed, ns = n√P / H5/4: Pelton wheels have low specific speeds, Francis runners medium, and Kaplan and propeller runners high. Low heads therefore need large, fast-turning runners passing huge flows, which is why run-of-river plants are physically big for their output.

Run-of-river, storage and pumped storage

A run-of-river plant uses the river as it comes. It floods little land, but its output rises and falls with the seasons. A storage dam keeps months of rain in its reservoir and can release it when electricity is needed: it is renewable power you can switch on, which makes it a natural partner for wind and solar.

Pumped storage adds a second reservoir. When power is plentiful, the turbines run backwards as pumps and lift water uphill; when it is scarce, the water runs back down. It stores energy rather than making it, and it is by far the largest form of grid storage today. More on the energy storage page.

Energy stored by lifting water

E = \rho\,V\,g\,H
E
energy, joules (divide by 3.6 × 10⁶ for kWh)
V
volume of water lifted, cubic metres
Lifting one million cubic metres (a lake 1 km × 1 km × 1 m) by 300 m stores 2.94 × 10¹² J, about 817 MWh before losses: enough to deliver 100 MW for about eight hours.

Strengths, limits and where hydro fits

Strengths

  • Very efficient, and plants run for many decades.
  • Storage dams can be dispatched: they ramp up and down within minutes to follow demand or cover a lull in the wind.
  • Pumped storage is the largest grid-scale store of energy in the world.
  • Reservoirs can also supply water, irrigation and flood control.

Limits

  • Depends on rain and snow: droughts cut output, and climate change makes river flows less predictable. Current consensus
  • Large dams flood valleys, can displace communities, block fish migration and change rivers downstream.
  • Most of the best sites in rich countries are already used; much of the remaining potential is in Africa, Asia and South America.
  • Big projects take many years to plan and build.

Hydro's flexibility is often worth more than its energy. In a grid with lots of wind and solar, a reservoir can hold its water back on sunny, windy days and run hard in the evening peak.
