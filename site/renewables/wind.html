Wind

Wind is sunlight once removed: the Sun heats the ground unevenly, air moves to even things out, and a turbine borrows some of that motion. Its blades are wings, not paddles, and the power they can catch grows with the cube of the wind speed.

A wind farm turns moving air into electricity with no fuel and no exhaust. By the end of 2025 the world had about 1,290 gigawatts of wind turbines, roughly 7 % of them at sea. Onshore wind was the cheapest new source of electricity in IRENA's 2024 cost survey, at a global average of about 3.4 US cents per kilowatt-hour. From a Scottish garden to 300-metre rotors

  1. 1887

    James Blyth lights his cottage. The Glasgow professor builds a cloth-sailed turbine about 8 m across that charges batteries for his lights, probably the first wind turbine to make electricity in Britain.

  2. 1888

    Charles Brush's giant. In Cleveland, Ohio, a 17 m rotor with 144 thin blades drives a 12 kW dynamo for twenty years. Many slow blades turn out to be the wrong idea for electricity.

  3. 1891

    Poul la Cour's test turbine. The Danish teacher builds an electricity-making wind turbine at Askov. From 1896 he tests blade shapes in small wind tunnels and finds that a few fast, well-shaped blades beat many slow ones.

  4. 1941

    The first megawatt. The Smith–Putnam turbine on Grandpa's Knob, Vermont, has a two-bladed rotor 53 m across and a 1.25 MW generator. It feeds the grid from October 1941 until a blade breaks off, and wartime shortages stop it being rebuilt.

  5. 1957

    The Gedser design. Johannes Juul's 200 kW, 24 m, three-bladed turbine in Denmark sets the pattern that commercial turbines follow from the 1980s.

  6. 1991

    Wind goes to sea. Vindeby, off Denmark, is the first offshore wind farm: eleven 0.45 MW turbines. In 25 years it makes 243 GWh, about what one modern offshore turbine now makes in a few years.

  7. 2021

    15 MW on one tower. Vestas launches the V236-15.0 MW, with a rotor 236 m across that sweeps more than four football pitches of air.

  8. 2025

    26 MW test turbine. Dongfang Electric installs a 26 MW prototype with a 310 m rotor at a test site in Shandong, China, according to trade press reports. Prototype 2026-10-02

How a blade turns wind into torque

A turbine blade works like an aeroplane wing turned on its side. Air flowing past a wing at a slight angle, the angle of attack, produces lift: a push at right angles to the flow. On a turbine, the trick is that the flow the blade feels is not the wind itself.

As the blade sweeps round, it rushes through the air, and that motion makes a headwind of its own, just as a cyclist feels wind on a still day. The blade feels the real wind and its own headwind together: the apparent wind. Near the tips the blade moves several times faster than the wind, so the apparent wind comes almost side-on. Lift, at right angles to that, then points mostly downwind, but with a small forward lean, and that lean is what turns the rotor.

Three things to try. First, stop the rotor: the apparent wind is just the wind, hitting the blade almost face-on, and the blade stalls. A parked rotor gets little torque, which is why big turbines are nudged up to speed. Second, raise the wind without changing rotor speed: the angle of attack grows until the blade stalls. Third, press Feather: the blade turns edge-on to the apparent wind and the force that drives the rotor almost vanishes. Modern turbines use blade pitch exactly like this, every second, to hold power steady in strong winds and to stop in storms.

Each slice of blade sees an inflow angle φ from the rotor plane with tan φ = v(1 − a)/(ω r), where a is the axial induction factor. The chord sits at θ = twist + pitch, so α = φ − θ. Because φ shrinks towards the tip, blades are built with twist: steep at the root, nearly flat at the tip. The drawing uses a twist that gives α = 6° along the whole blade at a tip-speed ratio of 8 with a = 1/3, and a thin-aerofoil lift slope of 2π per radian up to a stall near 14°. Resolving lift L and drag D into the rotor plane gives the driving force per unit span, L sin φ − D cos φ; the thrust is L cos φ + D sin φ. This is the blade-element model; real design adds the momentum balance on each annulus and tip-loss corrections.

How much power is in the wind

The cube law

The energy of moving air is its kinetic energy, ½mv². The faster the wind, the more air arrives each second as well: the mass flow through the rotor is ρAv. Multiply the two and the speed appears three times. Double the wind speed and the power in it rises eight-fold. This is why turbines climb ever taller, into faster wind, and why a site with a 20 % higher average wind is worth far more than 20 % more.

P_{\text{wind}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\rho\,A\,v^{3}, \qquad P_{\text{turbine}} = C_p\,P_{\text{wind}}
P
power, watts (W)
\rho
air density, about 1.225 kg/m³ at sea level and 15 °C
A
area swept by the rotor, π(D/2)², square metres
v
wind speed, metres per second
C_p
power coefficient: the share the rotor captures (no units)
A Vestas V236 sweeps 43,742 m². In a 10 m/s wind, ½ × 1.225 × 43,742 × 10³ = 26.8 MW of air power passes through its rotor.
A good modern rotor captures about 45 % of that (Cp ≈ 0.45), giving 12.1 MW. At 5 m/s it would be one eighth of that, 1.5 MW.

The Betz limit

Why only 45 %? A rotor cannot stop the wind dead: the air has to flow away behind it, or no more could arrive. Slow it too little and most of the energy passes straight through; slow it too much and air piles up and spills round the rotor. The best compromise is to slow the wind by one third at the rotor. Then the turbine catches 16/27 of the power, about 59.3 %. This is the Betz limit, found independently by Lanchester, Betz and Joukowsky around 1915–1920. Settled physics

C_p(a) = 4a(1-a)^2 \;\le\; C_p\!\left(\tfrac13\right) = \tfrac{16}{27} \approx 0.593
a
axial induction factor: the fraction by which the rotor slows the wind at the disc (no units)
C_p
power coefficient (no units)
With the wind slowed by one third at the rotor (a = 1/3), Cp = 4 × ⅓ × (⅔)² = 16/27 = 0.593. Real rotors lose a little more to blade drag, tip vortices and the swirl left in the wake.

Deriving it from the actuator disc

Treat the rotor as a thin disc of area A in a stream tube. Far upstream the wind is v; at the disc it is v(1 − a); far downstream, v(1 − 2a) (Bernoulli on each side of the disc plus a momentum balance gives the factor 2). The mass flow is ṁ = ρAv(1 − a). The thrust is the momentum lost, T = ṁ[v − v(1 − 2a)] = 2ρAv²a(1 − a). The power is thrust times the speed at the disc:

P = T\,v(1-a) = 2\rho A v^{3} a(1-a)^{2} = \tfrac12\rho A v^{3}\,\underbrace{4a(1-a)^{2}}_{C_p}

Setting dCp/da = 4(1 − a)(1 − 3a) = 0 gives a = 1/3 and Cp = 16/27. The model ignores wake rotation and blade count; including rotation lowers the ceiling slightly at low tip-speed ratios.

Power coefficient against induction factor

Cp rises from 0 at a = 0 to its peak of 0.593 at a = 1/3, then falls to 0.5 at a = 0.5. Beyond a = 0.5 the simple model breaks down (the wake would have to flow backwards).

Tip-speed ratio

How fast should the rotor spin? Too slow and wind slips between the blades; too fast and each blade churns air the one before has already slowed. Three-bladed rotors do best when the tips move about seven to nine times faster than the wind.

\lambda = \frac{\omega R}{v} = \frac{2\pi\,N R}{60\,v}
\lambda
tip-speed ratio (no units)
\omega,\ N
rotor speed, radians per second, or N revolutions per minute
R
rotor radius, metres
A 236 m rotor (R = 118 m) turning at a stately 7 rpm moves its tips at 86 m/s, over 300 km/h. In a 10 m/s wind that is λ = 8.65.

The flat top of the curve is deliberate. Strong winds are rare, so a generator big enough to use them would sit idle most of the year. Designers pick a rated power that the turbine reaches around 10–12 m/s and pitch the blades to spill the rest. The capacity factor, the year's energy divided by what the turbine would make at full power all year, comes from combining this curve with how often each wind speed blows. A Rayleigh distribution is a common first guess for that.

A 15 MW turbine with cut-in at 3 m/s, rated power from 10.8 m/s and cut-out at 31 m/s, at a site with Rayleigh winds averaging 9 m/s, has a capacity factor of 52 %: about 68 GWh a year. Vestas quotes capacity factors above 60 % for the V236 at the best sites.

The turbine presets use the published rotor diameter and rating; cut-in and cut-out are from the manufacturer where given (V172: 3 and 25 m/s; V236: 3 and 31 m/s) and otherwise assumed at 3 and 25 m/s. The rated wind speed in the model is where a rotor with Cp = 0.45 first reaches its rating, so these curves are illustrations, not certified power curves.

Strengths, limits and where wind fits

Strengths

  • No fuel and no combustion: once built, the energy is free.
  • Among the cheapest new sources of electricity on good sites.
  • Often blows hardest in winter and at night, when solar is weak, so the two complement each other in many climates.
  • Offshore turbines reach steadier, stronger winds and can be very large.

Limits

  • Variable: output follows the weather, and calm spells can last days. Current consensus
  • The cube law makes output very sensitive to site and to forecasting errors.
  • Large structures: visual impact, noise near homes, effects on birds, bats and sea life must be planned around.
  • Good sites are often far from cities, so new transmission lines are needed.

Wind does its best work as part of a system: spread over a wide area so local lulls cancel out, paired with solar, and backed by storage, hydro and flexible demand on a well-connected grid.
