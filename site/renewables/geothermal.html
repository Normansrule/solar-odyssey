Geothermal

Almost every other renewable is sunlight in disguise. Geothermal is not: it taps heat left over from the Earth's formation and released by radioactive decay deep inside it. Drill into hot rock, bring up hot water, make steam, spin a turbine, and send the cooled water back down.

of the world's electricity came from geothermal (counted together with the much smaller tidal and wave) in 2025: 89 TWh. 2026-09-30

Where it matters most, among the countries in this course's dataset:

A small slice worldwide, but a backbone where the rock is hot near the surface: along plate boundaries and volcanic zones such as Iceland, Kenya's Rift Valley, Indonesia, New Zealand and California. The world had about 16 gigawatts of geothermal power plants at the end of 2025. Unlike wind and solar, a geothermal plant can run day and night, whatever the weather.

From five light bulbs to engineered reservoirs

  1. 1904

    Larderello, Italy. Prince Piero Ginori Conti lights five bulbs with a small generator driven by natural steam from the ground.

  2. 1913

    The first geothermal power plant. Larderello 1 runs a 250 kW turbine on natural steam.

  3. 1930

    Reykjavík's hot water. Iceland's capital starts heating buildings with geothermal water. Today about 90 % of Iceland's space heating is geothermal.

  4. 1958

    Wairākei, New Zealand. The first plant to run on a hot-water (wet) field rather than dry steam, by flashing the water to steam. It still runs, at 133 MW.

  5. 1960

    The Geysers, California. Unit 1, on a dry-steam field like Larderello's, is the first modern geothermal power plant in the United States.

  6. 1967

    The binary cycle. Paratunka, in Russia, is the first plant to use an organic working fluid, opening up cooler resources.

  7. 2023

    Enhanced geothermal on the grid. Fervo Energy's Project Red in Nevada starts sending power to the grid from a reservoir engineered in hot rock: about 2 MW gross, 1.4 MW net. Active research

  8. 2024

    Utah FORGE. The U.S. Department of Energy's field laboratory connects two deep wells through stimulated granite and circulates water out at about 139 °C.

  9. 2026

    Cape Station. Fervo reports first power on 24 September from a 33 MW block of its Utah project, which it describes as the first utility-scale enhanced geothermal development to reach that milestone, and commercial operation on 30 September. 2026-10-02

How a geothermal plant works

A production well, often 1 to 3 km deep, taps a reservoir of hot water held in cracked rock. The hot fluid rises to the surface plant. There are three ways to turn it into electricity.

  • A dry steam plant uses steam straight from the ground, as at Larderello and The Geysers. Such fields are rare.
  • A flash plant takes water hotter than about 182 °C, held liquid by pressure, and lets the pressure drop so part of it boils instantly. This is the most common kind.
  • A binary plant passes cooler brine through a heat exchanger to boil a second fluid with a low boiling point. The brine never touches the turbine.

Either way, the cooled water goes back down a reinjection well. That keeps up the pressure in the reservoir and disposes of the dissolved minerals.

The geothermal loop

Hot water rises up the production well, gives up heat at the surface plant, and returns down the reinjection well. Change the reservoir temperature and switch between a flash and a binary plant.

Cooling the brine further extracts more heat, but dissolved minerals such as silica start to deposit in pipes.

Real plants span about 1 % to 21 %; the world average is about 12 %.

Why so little of the heat becomes electricity

The Carnot ceiling

Any engine that turns heat into work must dump some heat at a lower temperature; that is the second law of thermodynamics. The bigger the gap between hot and cold, the larger the share of heat that can become work. Geothermal fluid is hot, but much cooler than the steam in a modern fossil-fuel plant, so the ceiling is low. Settled physics

\eta_{\text{Carnot}} = 1 - \frac{T_c}{T_h}
\eta_{\text{Carnot}}
Carnot efficiency, the most any heat engine could achieve (no units)
T_h
temperature of the heat source, kelvin (°C + 273.15)
T_c
temperature where waste heat is dumped, kelvin
A 250 °C reservoir (523 K) and a 40 °C condenser (313 K): 1 − 313.15/523.15 = 40 % at the very most.

Real plants reach far less. A review of 94 plants worldwide found an average conversion efficiency of about 12 %, from around 21 % at the best dry-steam field down to about 1 % for binary plants on lukewarm brine. Part of the gap is ordinary friction and pumping. Part of it is unavoidable, as the expert note explains.

The cooling-stream ceiling

Carnot's formula assumes heat arrives at one fixed temperature. Geothermal brine does not: it gives up its heat while cooling from Th to Tout, so most of the heat comes in at temperatures below Th. Integrating the Carnot efficiency over the cooling stream, dW = (1 − Tc/T) m c dT, gives

\eta_{\max} = 1 - \frac{T_c \ln(T_h/T_{\text{out}})}{T_h - T_{\text{out}}}
Brine cooled from 250 °C to 80 °C with a 40 °C condenser can turn at most 27.6 % of the heat it gives up into work, well below the 40 % Carnot figure. Lowering Tout extracts more heat but at a lower average efficiency, so designers optimise the product, the net power.

How much heat the water carries

The heat drawn from the rock depends on how much water flows and how much it cools.

\dot Q = \dot m\,c_p\,\Delta T
\dot Q
heat flow, watts
\dot m
mass flow of water, kilograms per second
c_p
specific heat of water, about 4,200 J/(kg·K) (somewhat more for very hot water)
\Delta T
temperature drop between the production and reinjection wells, kelvin
A binary plant cooling 100 kg/s of brine from 150 °C to 70 °C draws 33.6 MW of heat. Even at the world-average 12 % that would be about 4 MW of electricity, and low-temperature binary plants usually manage less.

Enhanced geothermal: making reservoirs

Hot rock lies under every continent, but most of it is too tight for water to flow through. An enhanced geothermal system (EGS) creates the missing cracks by pumping water down under carefully controlled pressure, then circulates water between wells through the new fracture network. Active research

Results since 2023 are encouraging: Fervo's Nevada pilot has run for more than 600 days with little sign of cooling, Utah FORGE has shown that stimulated wells can be connected, and the first commercial-scale EGS block reached the grid in September 2026. Open questions remain about cost, long-term heat decline and induced earthquakes, which FORGE monitors closely; its largest event during the 2024 tests was magnitude 1.9, too small to feel. Most of the Fervo figures come from the company itself, so treat them as early reports. 2026-10-02

The key performance numbers for an EGS project are the flow rate per well pair, the production temperature, and how fast that temperature falls as cold injected water sweeps the fractures (thermal drawdown). Fervo reports about 2.1 MW gross (about 1.4 MW net) from Project Red, from roughly 36 kg/s at a production temperature near 175 °C: a modest flow, at a temperature suited to a binary plant.

Strengths, limits and where geothermal fits

Strengths

  • Runs around the clock in any weather: firm, clean power that complements wind and solar.
  • Small land footprint for the energy produced.
  • Heat can be used directly for district heating, greenhouses and industry, as in Iceland.

Limits

  • Conventional plants need rare places where hot water sits close to the surface.
  • Drilling is expensive and risky: a well may find less heat or flow than hoped.
  • Low conversion efficiency, and some fields release gases such as hydrogen sulfide and carbon dioxide from the brine.
  • Reservoirs can cool or lose pressure if they are worked too hard; reinjection helps.

Geothermal is one of the few clean sources that runs whenever it is needed, which makes it valuable in a grid dominated by variable wind and solar: it reduces how much storage and backup the grid needs.
