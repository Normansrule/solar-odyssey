Biomass

A log on the fire is sunlight from the last few decades, packed into sugar and wood by a plant. Burn it, rot it or ferment it and you get the energy back. Whether that helps the climate depends on what grows back afterwards.

2.2 %

of the world's electricity came from bioenergy in 2025: 710 TWh. 2026-09-30

Electricity is only part of bioenergy's story: far more is burned for heat and cooking or made into transport fuels. See State of the field for current figures.

Biomass is any plant or animal material used as fuel: wood and wood pellets, straw and husks, manure, food waste, and crops grown for energy. All of it got its energy from the same place as a solar panel, the Sun, through photosynthesis. Unlike a solar panel, a plant stores what it catches, so biomass can be burned when it is needed. The price is efficiency: plants are poor solar collectors.

From a Nobel prize to the carbon debt

  1. 1961

    How plants capture carbon. Melvin Calvin wins the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for working out how plants take carbon dioxide from the air and build it into sugars, the chemistry every form of biomass energy starts from.

  2. 1975

    Brazil bets on sugarcane. Brazil launches its National Alcohol Programme (Pró-Álcool) to replace petrol with ethanol from sugarcane.

  3. 2003

    Cars that take either fuel. Flexible-fuel cars, which run on any mix of petrol and ethanol, go on sale in Brazil.

  4. 2008

    The carbon debt. A study in Science shows that clearing rainforest, peatland or grassland to grow biofuel crops releases far more carbon than the biofuel saves each year: a debt that takes a long time to repay.

  5. 2008

    The ceiling on leaves. Plant scientists calculate the most a plant could ever store: 4.6 % of the sunlight for most crops (C3 plants) and 6 % for maize and sugarcane (C4 plants).

  6. 2011

    Leaves against panels. A cross-disciplinary team compares photosynthesis with solar cells on equal terms and finds solar-powered electrolysis about ten times more efficient over a year than crops. The same year, an ethanol plant in Decatur, Illinois, starts capturing its CO₂ and storing it underground.

  7. 2017

    BECCS at commercial scale. The Illinois project's commercial phase begins, able to handle up to 2,000 tonnes of CO₂ a day, roughly 0.7 million tonnes a year: still one of very few large plants of its kind. Early deployment

  8. 2022

    The IPCC weighs the trade-offs. The UN's climate panel finds bioenergy and BECCS can be a large part of mitigation, but warns that poorly planned biomass production can harm biodiversity, water and food security.

Four ways to get the sunlight back

Biomass is chemical energy: sugars, starch, cellulose and oils that the plant made from carbon dioxide, water and light. There are four main routes to release it.

  • Burning. Wood chips, pellets or straw are burned in a boiler; the steam spins a turbine, just as in a coal plant. Some coal plants have been converted to burn wood pellets, and the waste heat can warm buildings.
  • Gasification. Heating biomass with too little air to burn it makes a fuel gas, mostly carbon monoxide and hydrogen, which can run an engine or be turned into liquid fuels.
  • Anaerobic digestion. Microbes in a sealed tank break down manure, sewage or food waste and give off biogas, mostly methane. The biogas runs an engine or is cleaned up and fed into the gas network.
  • Biofuels. Yeast ferments sugar from sugarcane or maize into ethanol; vegetable oils and waste fats become biodiesel. These mostly power vehicles rather than the grid.

The left loop is what people mean by calling biomass renewable: the carbon goes round and round. But it only closes if the land grows back as much carbon as was burned, and on a timescale that matters. Turn regrowth down to 50 % and the biomass plant adds half as much carbon as coal, harvest after harvest. Tick the forest-clearing box and the biomass plant starts its life in debt. Turn on capture with full regrowth and the loop runs backwards: carbon leaves the air. That is the promise of BECCS. Current consensus

The model counts carbon only at the chimney and in regrowth. A real lifecycle assessment also counts the fossil energy used to farm, fertilise, dry, pellet and ship the fuel, the nitrous oxide from fertiliser, the methane from digesters that leak, and what the land would have stored if it had been left alone (the counterfactual). Those terms can be larger than the chimney term, which is why published lifecycle emissions for bioenergy span such a wide range.

The numbers behind a leaf

A plant as a solar collector

Think of a field as a solar panel that stores its output as chemical energy. The energy it stores in a year is the sunlight falling on it times the fraction it keeps. Plants waste most of the light: they use only part of the spectrum, they saturate in bright sun, they spend energy on breathing and growing, and fields are bare for part of the year. Even the theoretical best is 4.6 % for most crops and 6 % for C4 plants such as maize and sugarcane; in real fields, crops typically keep about 1 % of the year's sunlight.

E = H\,\eta_{\text{ps}}\,A
E
chemical energy stored in a year, kilowatt-hours (kWh)
H
yearly sunlight on the ground, kWh per square metre per year
\eta_{\text{ps}}
photosynthetic efficiency: share of the sunlight stored (no units)
A
area, square metres (m²)
A sunny mid-latitude site receives about 1,800 kWh/m² a year (an assumed round figure). At 1 %, one square metre of crop stores 1,800 × 0.01 = 18 kWh a year as chemical energy.
Burn that crop in a power station that turns an assumed 30 % of the fuel's heat into electricity, and the whole chain from sunlight to electricity is 0.01 × 0.30 = 0.003, or 0.3 %. A solar panel on the same square metre would deliver tens of times more electricity. Biomass earns its place by being storable and by using wastes, not by efficiency.

Where the light goes

Photosynthesis builds sugar from carbon dioxide and water: 6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O + light → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂. Zhu, Long and Ort follow a beam of sunlight down the chain: about half of the solar energy lies outside the 400–700 nm band that chlorophyll uses; some is reflected or passes through the leaf; each absorbed photon's energy above the reaction centres' needs is lost as heat; then the carbon-fixing enzyme Rubisco also grabs oxygen by mistake (photorespiration), which C4 plants largely avoid by concentrating CO₂; finally the plant respires part of what it made. Multiplying these stages gives the 4.6 % (C3, 30 °C, 380 ppm CO₂) and 6 % (C4) ceilings. Their analysis also notes that C4's advantage shrinks as atmospheric CO₂ rises.

From carbon to carbon dioxide

When biomass or coal burns, each carbon atom picks up two oxygen atoms from the air. Oxygen is heavy, so the carbon dioxide weighs more than the carbon did: 12 parts of carbon become 44 parts of CO₂. That is why a tonne of fuel can produce well over a tonne of CO₂.

m_{\mathrm{CO_2}} = m_{\mathrm{C}} \times \frac{M_{\mathrm{CO_2}}}{M_{\mathrm{C}}} = m_{\mathrm{C}} \times \frac{44.009}{12.011}
m
mass, kilograms or tonnes
M
molar mass, grams per mole: carbon 12.011, carbon dioxide 44.009
Burning 1 kg of carbon releases 1 × 44.009 ÷ 12.011 = 3.66 kg of CO₂. In the carbon-loop model above, one harvest burns a tonne of carbon: 3.66 tonnes of CO₂.

Is it good for the climate? The honest answer: it depends

Few energy sources divide experts like biomass. The physics is not in dispute; the bookkeeping is.

  • Current consensus Wastes and residues are the low-risk end. Using crop residues, sawmill waste, manure and food waste that would otherwise rot or be burned in the open takes no extra land. Dedicated energy crops have a much larger potential, but also larger risks: the IPCC puts the technical potential of residues at roughly 5–50 exajoules a year by 2050, and of dedicated biomass at 50–250, with medium agreement.
  • Current consensus Land is the crux. Clearing forest, peatland or grassland for energy crops releases stored carbon that can outweigh the fossil fuel saved for many years. The IPCC finds with high confidence that poorly planned biomass production can conflict with biodiversity, water, food security and livelihoods.
  • Active research BECCS at scale is unproven. Capturing CO₂ from bioenergy plants and storing it underground could remove carbon from the air. The IPCC estimates a technical potential of about 5.9 (range 0.5–11.3) billion tonnes of CO₂ a year, of which 1.6 billion is available below 100 US dollars per tonne. Operating projects remain few and small next to those numbers: the best known, at an ethanol plant in Decatur, Illinois, had stored about 3.4 million tonnes in total by 2021.
  • Settled Plants are inefficient collectors. On the same land, solar panels capture far more energy per year than crops. Biomass is worth its land mainly where it does something panels cannot: store energy for months, make liquid fuels and materials, or use waste.

Strengths, limits and where biomass fits

Strengths

  • Stored energy: a pile of pellets or a tank of biogas can run when wind and solar cannot.
  • Can reuse existing boilers, turbines, engines and gas networks.
  • Turns wastes that would rot (and emit methane) into useful energy.
  • With carbon capture, one of the few technologies that could remove CO₂ from the air while making energy.

Limits

  • Very low efficiency from sunlight, so it needs a lot of land.
  • Climate benefit depends on feedstock, land history and regrowth time; it can be worse than fossil fuel.
  • Competes with food, forests and biodiversity for land and water.
  • Burning causes local air pollution unless well controlled.
