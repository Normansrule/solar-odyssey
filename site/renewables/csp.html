Concentrated solar power

A solar cell turns light straight into electricity. Concentrated solar power takes the long way round: thousands of mirrors focus sunlight into heat, the heat boils water, and the steam spins a turbine. The detour has one big reward. Heat is easy to store, so the turbine can keep running after sunset.

Concentrated solar power (CSP) is a power station whose boiler is heated by the Sun. It needs strong, direct sunshine: the beam that casts sharp shadows, not the diffuse glow of a cloudy sky, because only a direct beam can be focused. That restricts it to deserts and dry, sunny regions such as southern Spain, Morocco, South Africa, western China and the American Southwest.

A century of mirrors

  1. 1913

    Pumping the Nile with sunshine. Frank Shuman and C. V. Boys run a solar steam plant at Meadi, near Cairo: rows of 62 m parabolic troughs, about 1,200 m² of mirror, driving an irrigation pump at 37–45 kW. The First World War and cheap fuel shut it down by 1915.

  2. 1982

    Solar One. A 10 MW power tower near Barstow, California, runs until 1988 and proves that a field of steerable mirrors can feed a utility grid.

  3. 1984

    SEGS, the trough era. The first of nine Solar Energy Generating Systems opens at Daggett, California, a 13.8 MW parabolic-trough plant. The nine together reach 354 MW and remain the world's largest solar thermal facility until 2014.

  4. 1996

    Salt as a battery. Solar One is rebuilt as Solar Two, using molten nitrate salt at about 565 °C both to collect heat and to store three hours of it.

  5. 2011

    Running through the night. Gemasolar, near Seville, opens: a 20 MW tower with 2,650 heliostats and 15 hours of molten-salt storage, enough to run round the clock in summer.

  6. 2014

    Ivanpah. Three power towers in the Mojave Desert begin generating and overtake SEGS as the world's largest solar thermal facility. They store no heat.

  7. 2018

    Noor III. A 150 MW molten-salt tower 247 m tall, with seven hours of storage, enters service at Ouarzazate, Morocco.

  8. 2025

    A warning from the desert. California's largest utility agrees to end its Ivanpah contracts early, saying cheaper alternatives will save customers money. Photovoltaics with batteries now compete head-on with CSP's selling point, stored solar energy. 2026-09-30

Four ways to focus the Sun

  • Parabolic trough. Long curved mirrors focus sunlight onto a pipe of oil running along the focal line, heating it. The whole row tilts during the day to follow the Sun. The most built design.
  • Power tower. A field of flat, steerable mirrors, heliostats, sends sunlight up to a receiver on a tower. Higher concentration means hotter salt (around 565 °C) and a more efficient turbine.
  • Linear Fresnel. Rows of nearly flat mirror strips, each tilted a little differently, act like one big trough and focus onto a fixed pipe overhead. Cheaper to build, less efficient.
  • Dish. A dish focuses onto a single point, reaching the highest concentrations, often with a small engine at the focus. Built only in small numbers.

Two things stand out. Mirrors on the side of the tower away from the Sun do best: in the northern hemisphere the Sun is mostly in the south, so a mirror north of the tower faces it almost squarely, while a mirror south of the tower must turn its back to the Sun at a slant to reach the tower. That is why the field is drawn, like real ones at this latitude, with more mirrors to the north. And at sunrise and sunset the whole field collects little, because the low Sun meets most mirrors obliquely and its light has crossed more atmosphere.

Cosine loss is only one of the field's losses. Real fields also lose light to mirror soiling and imperfect reflectivity, shading and blocking between neighbouring mirrors, atmospheric attenuation over hundreds of metres, and spillage around the receiver.

The equations of focused sunlight

Concentration ratio

A magnifying glass can light paper because it gathers sunlight from a large area into a small spot. The concentration ratio says how many suns' worth of light land on the receiver: mirror area over receiver area. More concentration means a hotter receiver, and less of it, so it loses less heat.

C = \frac{A_{\text{aperture}}}{A_{\text{receiver}}}
C
geometric concentration ratio (no units; "suns")
A_{\text{aperture}}
total mirror area facing the Sun, m²
A_{\text{receiver}}
area of the receiver the light lands on, m²
An illustrative tower: 1,000 heliostats of 120 m² each (120,000 m² of mirror) aimed at a 150 m² receiver concentrate the light 800 times. A parabolic trough, focusing in only one direction, manages far less.

The Carnot ceiling

Once sunlight has become heat, CSP is a heat engine, and every heat engine obeys the same rule: it can only turn part of its heat into work, and the hotter the heat compared with the surroundings, the larger that part. This is why towers, which run hotter, can beat troughs.

\eta_{\text{Carnot}} = 1 - \frac{T_{\text{cold}}}{T_{\text{hot}}}
\eta_{\text{Carnot}}
Carnot efficiency: the highest possible share of heat turned into work (no units)
T_{\text{hot}},\ T_{\text{cold}}
temperatures of the heat source and of the surroundings that take the waste heat, in kelvin (°C + 273.15)
Salt at 565 °C (838 K), as at Gemasolar, with heat rejected at an assumed 30 °C (303 K): 1 − 303.15 ÷ 838.15 = 0.638. No engine could turn more than 64 % of that heat into electricity; real steam cycles, with their own losses, get well under that.

Storing heat in molten salt

CSP stores energy the simplest way possible: make something hot and keep it hot. A two-tank plant pumps salt from a "cold" tank (still around 290 °C) through the receiver into a hot tank at 565 °C; at night it pumps the hot salt through a steam generator and back. The heat stored is sensible heat: mass times specific heat times the temperature rise.

E = m\,c_p\,\Delta T
E
heat stored, joules (J); divide by 3.6 × 10⁹ for megawatt-hours
m
mass of molten salt, kg
c_p
specific heat capacity, J/(kg·K); about 1,500 for nitrate "solar salt" (the round value this site's model uses)
\Delta T
temperature difference between the hot and cold tanks, K
1,000 tonnes of salt (10⁶ kg) heated from 290 °C to 565 °C (ΔT = 275 K) store 10⁶ × 1,500 × 275 = 4.1 × 10¹¹ J, or 115 MWh of heat. At an assumed 40 % steam-cycle efficiency that is 46 MWh of electricity: about 2.3 hours for a 20 MW turbine.

Strengths, limits and where CSP fits

Strengths

  • Built-in, cheap storage: heat in a tank of salt, enough for many hours.
  • Spinning turbines give the grid inertia, like conventional plants.
  • High-temperature heat can also serve industry directly.

Limits

  • Needs strong direct sunlight; useless under cloud or haze.
  • Large, complex plants with many moving mirrors and hot fluids.
  • Costs have fallen more slowly than those of solar panels and batteries, which now compete with it for evening power. Current consensus
